# AI Occupational Exposure Index (AIOE) and Task Routinization Index

**Summary**: The two quantitative frameworks — Felten, Raj & Seamans' AIOE and Acemoglu & Autor's Task Routinization Index — that the ERO/Advance CTE/ACTE "Fostering AI-Ready CTE Pipelines" report series uses to measure AI's occupational exposure across all six Career Cluster Groupings.

## Two complementary measures

Both measures are applied identically across all four "Fostering AI-Ready CTE Pipelines" reports ([[fostering-ai-ready-building-moving]], [[fostering-ai-ready-cultivating-resources]], [[fostering-ai-ready-caring-communities]], [[fostering-ai-ready-creating-experiencing]]), letting the four reports be compared directly on the same scale.

### AIOE: a single occupational score

The **Artificial Intelligence Occupational Exposure (AIOE) index** (Felten, Raj & Seamans, 2021, *Strategic Management Journal* 42(12)) quantifies the degree to which AI's capabilities overlap with the tasks and skills of a specific occupation, based on O*NET occupational skills data. The index is standardized to a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1 across all US occupations. A higher AIOE score means greater occupational exposure to AI; a lower (or negative) score means less exposure. AIOE is *correlational*, not predictive — a high score indicates overlap between what AI can do and what the occupation requires, not a forecast that the job will be automated (source: all four reports, citing Felten et al., 2021).

Felten et al. (2021, p. 2203) found that the most AI-exposed industries are white-collar and require high levels of education — financial services, accounting, insurance, legal services — while the lowest-scoring industries are blue-collar and manual — crop production support, building services, construction contracting, warehousing and storage.

### Task Routinization Index: task-level granularity

The **Task Routinization Index** (Acemoglu & Autor, 2011, *Handbook of Labor Economics*) provides more granular insight than AIOE's single score. It decomposes each occupation into four task categories — Routine Manual, Non-Routine Manual, Routine Cognitive, Non-Routine Cognitive — using O*NET task-importance data, and scores each category from 1 (not important) to 5 (very important) for that occupation. This reveals *what kind* of exposure an occupation has, not just how much.

## The consistent finding across all four reports

Every one of the four reports, covering four of the six Career Cluster Groupings, reaches the same core conclusion using this methodology:

1. **AI correlates most strongly with cognitive tasks, not physical ones.** AI has "minimal impact on jobs requiring significant physical abilities but strongly affects roles involving information processing and cognitive functions" (Felten et al., 2021, p. 2203, quoted in [[fostering-ai-ready-caring-communities]]). This is why physicists show greater AI exposure than surgeons in the data — both require cognitive skill, but surgery also depends on physical, psychomotor, and sensory abilities that current AI cannot replicate.
2. **Exposure rises with the education level required for entry.** In every cluster grouping studied — Building & Moving, Cultivating Resources, Caring for Communities, Creating & Experiencing — occupations requiring a bachelor's degree or higher show the highest AIOE scores; occupations requiring a high school diploma or less show the lowest. This pattern holds even within manual-labor-coded clusters like Construction and Agriculture, where the "high-skill" occupations (civil engineers, natural resources managers) are the AIOE outliers.
3. **A high AIOE score indicates likely AI *integration*, not automatic job loss.** All four reports explicitly reject the inference that AIOE predicts displacement. Historical precedent shows technology can eliminate routine-task-heavy roles, but elevated AIOE scores can equally signal that an occupation will need *more* human workers to develop, implement, supervise, and refine AI tools within that domain. The reports' formulation, repeated verbatim across all four: "where there are higher AIOE scores, there is a higher likelihood that AI will be integrated into those fields of work" — not necessarily higher likelihood of job elimination.
4. **Substitution vs. complementarity is the operative frame, not automation vs. no automation.** All four reports use the same task-based logic from Acemoglu & Autor (2011) and the U.S. GAO (2022): technology acts on individual tasks, and occupations only indirectly. When enough tasks within an occupation become substitutable, occupation-level automation risk rises — but the more common historical pattern is complementarity, where technology increases the value and demand for the human skills that remain.
5. **Technical skills are occupation-specific and more automatable; transferable skills cut across occupations and are less automatable.** Each report distinguishes technical skills (welding, diagnostic imaging interpretation, prompt engineering) from transferable skills (active listening, critical thinking, negotiation) and finds transferable skills consistently less exposed to AI substitution — directly reinforcing the wiki's [[transferable-skills]] thesis with occupation-level data across four additional cluster groupings.

## Cross-cluster-grouping comparison

Because all four reports use identical methodology, their Figure 1 charts can be read side-by-side as a rough map of AI exposure across the CTE landscape (excluding Managing & Connecting and Digital Technology, not yet covered by this report series as of mid-2026):

| Cluster Grouping | AIOE relative to national average | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Building & Moving (Advanced Manufacturing, Construction, Supply Chain & Transportation) | At or slightly below average | Lowest-exposure grouping studied; only bachelor's-level roles (engineers, construction managers) exceed the national average |
| Cultivating Resources (Agriculture, Energy & Natural Resources) | Close to average | Agriculture slightly below; Energy & Natural Resources slightly above; high-AIOE outliers concentrated in bachelor's-level science/management roles |
| Creating & Experiencing (Hospitality/Events/Tourism, Arts/Entertainment/Design) | Average, with an internal split | Arts, Entertainment & Design slightly above average; Hospitality, Events & Tourism slightly below — but flagged as still highly automation-vulnerable at the task level (see [[fostering-ai-ready-creating-experiencing]]) |
| Caring for Communities (Education, Healthcare & Human Services, Public Service & Safety) | Slightly above average (except Healthcare & Human Services) | Highest-exposure grouping studied; Education and Public Service & Safety both above the national mean, driven by teachers, lawyers, and administrators |

This ordering roughly tracks each grouping's average credential requirement and cognitive-task intensity — consistent with finding #2 above — and gives [[cte-and-ai]]'s "AI Exposure by CTE Cluster" section its first occupation-level empirical grounding, rather than a general impression.

## Limitations

- AIOE and the Task Routinization Index are both built from O*NET, a survey-based occupational database — they measure *task composition*, not real-world AI deployment or actual displacement rates.
- The four reports' Figure 1 charts plot averages across occupations within a cluster; individual occupations vary substantially (e.g., civil engineers and construction managers score well above the Construction cluster's average).
- None of the four reports track AIOE scores longitudinally — they are a single 2021 snapshot correlated against 2023 BLS employment data, not a forecast of how exposure will change as AI capabilities advance.
