# Augmented Leadership: Navigating the New Age of Intelligence (CEMS)

**Summary**: A 2026 report from CEMS — The Global Alliance in Management Education (33 business schools, 70+ corporate partners) — synthesizing interviews with corporate and academic leaders into ten workplace takeaways and thirty role-specific insights on AI-age leadership, business education, and early-career work. Not K-12/CTE-specific, but its findings on cognitive offloading, the widening early-career expertise gap, and "copilot not autopilot" closely corroborate the wiki's existing K-12 and CTE evidence base from the corporate/graduate-education side.

**Sources**: `raw/PDF/CEMS AI Report_ Augmented leadership_2026.pdf`

**Last updated**: 2026-07-02

---

## Overview

CEMS surveyed 20 multinational corporate partners and conducted in-depth interviews with seven contributors — corporate HR/strategy leaders (ABB, Beko, tesa), business school deans and professors (American University in Cairo, University of Sydney, Université Catholique de Louvain), and an AI strategist/entrepreneur — to define what "next-level digital fluency" means for business leaders, educators, and early-career professionals in 2030 and beyond. (source: CEMS AI Report_ Augmented leadership_2026.pdf)

The report is aimed at graduate business education and corporate leadership development, not K-12. It is included in this wiki because its conclusions — reached independently, from the employer/business-school side of the pipeline rather than the K-12 research side — substantially reproduce findings already established elsewhere in the wiki, providing cross-sector corroboration.

## Ten Key Takeaways

1. **GenAI redefines work, not headcount.** Most workers won't be replaced one-to-one, but tasks shift from writing/research/production toward reviewing, curating, and iterating.
2. **Cognitive offloading is a major risk.** Outsourcing small decisions (scheduling, writing, even meals) to AI erodes critical thinking. "Don't let AI be your master. Be AI's master."
3. **Thinking must precede prompting.** "Think first, prompt second" — bring your own ideas before turning to AI.
4. **AI is a tool, not a colleague.** It cannot be performance-managed, cannot learn continuously from conversation, and treating it as an autopilot rather than copilot is the central risk identified across every interview.
5. **Ethics is foundational, not a digital accessory** — source-checking, bias awareness, and knowing when to stop must become instinctive.
6. **AI should enhance creativity**, not replace it, by pushing beyond established models toward original thought.
7. **Human wisdom matters more as AI scales** — empathy, context, and self-awareness of one's own biases become the differentiators.
8. **AI is a continuous development tool** — but only for those who verify and interrogate its outputs rather than accepting them uncritically.
9. **The expertise gap is widening.** Early-career tasks that historically built judgment and intuition are increasingly automatable, undermining the "training ground" function of junior roles.
10. **Business schools are more critical training grounds than ever**, positioned to teach not just how AI systems work but when and why to use them.

## The Expertise Gap: Direct Corroboration of a CTE Concern

ABB's Guillaume Delacour makes the expertise-gap argument in stark terms: "It can take 10 to 15 years to build a high level of expertise, yet many of the foundational, early-career tasks that used to be critical to build the skills and experiences of junior professionals have the potential to be largely done by AI, at marginal cost. So if those tasks are automated, companies will have to rethink hard how to develop the next generation of experts." (source: CEMS AI Report_ Augmented leadership_2026.pdf)

This is the corporate-employer-side mirror of the wiki's [[broken-ladder]] and [[anthropic-labor-market-index]] findings on entry-level hiring contraction, and of [[brad-smith-ai-jobs-next-generation]]'s "perfect storm" framing — but reframed as a *skills pipeline* problem rather than a *hiring numbers* problem. Where those sources measure the hiring decline, CEMS's interviewees describe the mechanism from inside the org: junior roles aren't just being hired less, the tasks that used to *train* juniors are being automated out from under them.

## Cognitive Offloading, Positive AI Attitudes, and Metacognition: A Second Independent Confirmation

AI strategist Aleksandra Kjemhus names "cognitive offloading" as one of the era's central risks and frames the corrective habit explicitly: "Schedule periods of focused, independent thinking before engaging with AI. Treat it like a training routine: brain first, AI second." University of Sydney's Kai Riemer makes the same point from the assessment-design side: "Simply outsourcing thought is 'vibe working' — coasting on outputs. A graduate whose only skill is prompting a chatbot adds no value to a prospective employer as they could just ask the chatbot themselves." (source: CEMS AI Report_ Augmented leadership_2026.pdf)

This is a striking corroboration — via a completely different population (adult professionals and business-school interviewees, not middle schoolers) and a completely different methodology (qualitative interviews, not signal-detection experiments) — of [[illusion-of-understanding]]'s core empirical finding: that AI's fluent, confident output style suppresses the evaluative reflex needed to catch bad answers, and that only active metacognitive habit-building counteracts it. Riemer's "Truth 2" — that critical thinking and experience make AI use *more* effective, and that "the idea that digital natives will 'just figure it out' doesn't hold" — directly echoes the [[illusion-of-understanding]] finding that domain/experience-related factors, not youth or comfort with technology, predict good AI interaction quality.

## "Copilot, Not Autopilot": A Recurring Phrase Across Sources

Tesa CEO Dr. Kourosh Bahrami frames the report's central risk explicitly: "The greatest risk I see today is that people begin to treat AI as an autopilot rather than a copilot... The issue is not that AI will dominate us — it is that we may willingly hand over control because it feels easier." This is the same "autopilot vs. copilot" framing CEMS uses as its closing thesis: "Our collective mission is clear: to keep humans in control, judgment at the centre, and AI as a copilot, but never the autopilot." (source: CEMS AI Report_ Augmented leadership_2026.pdf)

This maps directly onto [[agentivism]]'s delegation/epistemic-monitoring framework and [[applied-co-intelligence-model]]'s emphasis on maintaining human judgment within AI-augmented workflows — the business-education literature and the K-12 learning-theory literature have converged on structurally identical language independently.

## Assessment Design: A Third Educational Institution Reaches the Same Conclusion

Université Catholique de Louvain's Evelyne Léonard describes redesigning master's thesis assessment around three AI-resistant competencies: (1) demonstrating one's own reasoning in structuring and solving a complex problem, (2) making one's personal intellectual contribution visible and distinguishable from AI-generated content, and (3) demonstrating project management skill. University of Sydney's Kai Riemer independently describes a "two-lane" system: one lane assumes AI use and asks for reflection on it, the other uses supervised oral/practical/written exams where AI cannot be used at all. (source: CEMS AI Report_ Augmented leadership_2026.pdf)

This is a third independent institutional convergence — alongside [[middle-path-ai-literacy-nurenberg]]'s AI-audit approach and [[ai-unplugged-murgatroyd]]'s "build, analyze, defend" scaffold — on oral/process-based assessment as the practical response to AI-assisted work. All three institutions, working independently across secondary and graduate education, arrived at variants of the same solution: shift assessment weight toward demonstrated process and defended reasoning rather than final-product evaluation.

## Léonard's Fairness Concern

Léonard raises an equity point not previously present in the wiki's assessment-design discussion: "Some students work tirelessly, while others lean heavily on AI. The latter may not achieve excellent marks, but they often receive decent grades with far less effort. For those who put in the hard work, this feels unjust." This fairness dimension — AI use compressing the grade distribution and rewarding effort-minimization — is a distinct concern from the *learning* harms documented in [[illusion-of-understanding]] and [[cognitive-debt]], and worth tracking separately as institutions design AI-era grading policy.

## The Four Buckets of Skills (Bahrami)

Tesa's Bahrami proposes four skill "buckets" for the AI age: **Digital Literacy** (classical AI, GenAI, agents, automation tools), **Critical Thinking** (extended to digital ethics and cybersecurity), **Interpersonal Skills** (collaboration, empathy, communication), and **Self-Mastery** (understanding one's own values, biases, and blind spots — described as "the most crucial" bucket). This four-part structure is a close corporate-sector analog to the wiki's [[applied-co-intelligence-model]] (Technical Skills × Transferable Skills × AI Mastery) and to [[transferable-skills]]'s argument that human competencies command growing economic value as technical execution is automated.

## A Note on Optimism

Unlike several of the wiki's more contested sources (e.g., [[fostering-ai-ready-creating-experiencing]]'s Jesse Anglen dissent), every CEMS interviewee expresses a consistently optimistic "augmentation, not automation" stance — this is a report produced by and for an alliance of business schools and corporate partners with a direct institutional interest in presenting AI as manageable and their graduates as well-positioned. Sherif Kamel (American University in Cairo) makes the optimism explicit: "unlike many, I remain optimistic... I believe that AI will be no different, if we embrace it wisely." This should be read as the report's institutional voice, not a neutral finding, and it stands in useful tension with the more empirically grounded caution of [[illusion-of-understanding]] and [[ai-economy-measurement-2026]] found elsewhere in the wiki.

## Implications for CTE

CEMS is a graduate business-education alliance, not a CTE or K-12 organization, so its direct applicability is limited — but three findings transfer:

1. **The expertise-gap mechanism (automated-away training tasks) applies to CTE apprenticeship models too.** If AI automates the low-stakes, repetitive tasks that traditionally build competence in an occupation (whether junior analyst work or entry-level trade tasks), CTE programs relying on "learn by doing the easy stuff first" progression may need to redesign around this same problem Delacour describes for corporate juniors.
2. **The oral/process-based assessment convergence strengthens the case for CTE programs already using demonstration-based assessment.** CTE's traditional emphasis on skills demonstration and practical exams is, per this report and [[middle-path-ai-literacy-nurenberg]]/[[ai-unplugged-murgatroyd]], structurally well-suited to the AI era relative to essay- or homework-based assessment models that other fields are now scrambling to redesign.
3. **"Copilot not autopilot" and cognitive-offloading language is now dominant across sectors** — K-12 research ([[illusion-of-understanding]]), corporate leadership development (this report), and CTE-specific frameworks ([[applied-co-intelligence-model]]) have converged on nearly identical framing, suggesting this is now a stable cross-sector consensus term rather than a niche academic concept.
