# Goldman Sachs: AI Displacement Will Track the Business Cycle

**Summary**: A Fortune report (October 2026) on a Goldman Sachs research note by economist Joseph Briggs. It forecasts that up to 9% of US workers could be displaced during the AI transition, but argues the timing will follow the business cycle: historically, technology-driven job losses are small in healthy economies and concentrated in recessions. This offers a strong explanation for why AI's labor effects look modest so far, and points to the next downturn as the real test.

## About this source

Fortune article (October 23, 2026, via archive.ph) reporting a Goldman Sachs note published October 22 by Joseph Briggs, who co-leads Goldman's global economics team (source: AI could displace one in 11 workers, Goldman says, but the layoffs ar….md). **This is a secondhand account of a sell-side research note**: the note itself is not in `raw/`, and its headline figure is a model-based forecast, not a measurement. It is a follow-up from the same bank as [[goldman-sachs-ai-labor-squeeze]] (August 2026), but a different analysis by a different team.

---

## The forecast: up to 9% displaced, but not mostly as unemployment

- **The headline.** If AI eventually raises productivity by 15% in the US and other developed economies, up to **9% of workers could be "displaced during the AI transition,"** with slower hiring weighing on employment by roughly another **3%** (source: AI could displace one in 11 workers, Goldman says, but the layoffs ar….md).
- **How the 9% is derived.** Historically, each 1% of technology-driven productivity gain raised the cumulative gross job-loss rate by 0.5–0.6 points; multiplying the top of that range by 15 gives 9. The same 1% gain pushed the unemployment rate up only 0.3–0.4 points at its peak, fading to zero after two years (source: AI could displace one in 11 workers, Goldman says, but the layoffs ar….md).
- **Gross job losses, not unemployment.** The note counts job losses because "the jobless rate hides people": some displaced workers find jobs quickly, while others "exit the labor force." Briggs has said that even with 9% displaced, "the unemployment rate increase in any given year would be less than one percentage point" (source: AI could displace one in 11 workers, Goldman says, but the layoffs ar….md).
- **The estimate has risen.** In August 2025 Briggs and Sarah Dong estimated 6–7% displacement (range 3–14%) under the same productivity assumption. By June 2026 Briggs put it at about 9%, or 15 million workers over a decade, at first describing them as "reallocated" and now as "displaced" (source: AI could displace one in 11 workers, Goldman says, but the layoffs ar….md).
- **Reasons it could be higher.** Displacement ran higher during the late-1990s/early-2000s productivity boom, "the period most widely viewed as comparable to today," and after unusually large productivity jumps. Goldman expects AI's boost to exceed most past ones (source: AI could displace one in 11 workers, Goldman says, but the layoffs ar….md).

## The central finding: displacement waits for recessions

- **Automation is cyclical.** "Automation-related labor disruption is limited when the overall economy is healthy but accelerates during cyclical slowdowns," with "companies tending to automate work and shed labor costs when the economy starts to slow" (source: AI could displace one in 11 workers, Goldman says, but the layoffs ar….md).
- **Routine jobs vanish in steps.** The share of Americans in routine jobs (clerks, secretaries, factory machine operators, mechanics) "has remained stable during expansions but declined sharply during downturns." It dropped in steps in the early-1980s, 2001, 2008, and 2020 recessions and never recovered (source: AI could displace one in 11 workers, Goldman says, but the layoffs ar….md).
- **Measured amplification.** The unemployment hit from a technology-driven productivity gain was 0.1–0.2 points larger when the jobless rate was rising. Across 25 occupations upended by past technology, employment fell an extra 4% over five years for every point the national unemployment rate rose (source: AI could displace one in 11 workers, Goldman says, but the layoffs ar….md).
- **Briggs's warning:** "a cyclical slowdown would likely accelerate AI-related automation and job losses, thereby amplifying the broader business cycle" (source: AI could displace one in 11 workers, Goldman says, but the layoffs ar….md).

Goldman's US economists (David Mericle and Pierfrancesco Mei) reached the same conclusion a year earlier, calling "jobless growth" the likely new normal and warning that "the full consequences of AI for the labor market might not become apparent until a recession hits." They pointed to the "jobless recovery" after the dot-com bust, when productivity and GDP rebounded before employment (source: AI could displace one in 11 workers, Goldman says, but the layoffs ar….md).

## What happened to occupations past technology hit hardest

Briggs studied 25 occupations, including photo processors, movie projectionists, proofreaders, file clerks, and bank tellers (source: AI could displace one in 11 workers, Goldman says, but the layoffs ar….md):
- Employment fell **25% on average** in the five years after decline began. Briggs calls these "likely upper bounds" for AI, since he chose the hardest-hit cases.
- **Most of the shrinkage came from separations, not reduced hiring.** Separation rates rose in 19 of 25 occupations (by 3.8 points on average); hiring rates fell in 13 (by 0.7 points). Briggs therefore treats AI-related layoffs as "a good real-time guide" to disruption.

**⚠ Tension with current AI evidence.** [[canaries-coal-mine-brynjolfsson]] finds today's AI effect on young workers operates through **reduced hiring, not increased separations** (source: Canaries_August2026.pdf), and [[ai-labor-demand-41-countries]] finds adopting firms reshaping by seniority without cutting headcount (source: HowDoesAIChangeLaborDemand.pdf). The historical pattern says displacement mainly takes the form of layoffs. The two fit together if AI is still in its "expansion phase": firms adjust quietly through hiring now, and the layoff-driven displacement history predicts would arrive in a downturn. That reconciliation is this wiki's inference, not a claim either source makes.

## Why the impact is limited so far

Briggs gives two reasons (source: AI could displace one in 11 workers, Goldman says, but the layoffs ar….md):
1. **It's early.** Only **23% of US establishments** have built AI into regular workflows, and most AI-enabled productivity gains are still ahead.
2. **The economy is resilient.** With a strong labor market in 2026, "companies may have had less incentive to focus on aggressively cutting labor costs."

Current readings (source: AI could displace one in 11 workers, Goldman says, but the layoffs ar….md):
- AI-attributed layoffs in Challenger's tracking have fallen sharply from their spring peak.
- Job growth in the most AI-exposed industries ran about **10,000 below trend** over the past three months, roughly **offset by hiring for AI-related construction**.
- Briggs: "a modest labor market headwind is emerging," but near-term headwinds "remain smaller than feared."

**A softened prominent voice.** Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, who in 2025 warned AI could eliminate half of entry-level white-collar jobs, invoked the Jevons paradox in May 2026: "If you automate 90% of the job, then everyone does the 10% of the job... And the 10% kind of expands to be 100% of what people do." He kept a caveat: "AI is moving faster than all these previous technologies... It's possible you get these weird behaviors and this big disruption" (source: AI could displace one in 11 workers, Goldman says, but the layoffs ar….md).

## Implications for CTE

1. **Plan for the downturn, not the current calm.** If displacement is held back in good times and released in recessions, today's quiet labor market is not evidence of safety. CTE programs and state systems should prepare retraining capacity for when a slowdown hits.
2. **Routine-task pathways are most exposed when the cycle turns.** Clerical and routine cognitive roles are exactly what disappeared in past recessions and did not come back. Pathways leading to such roles should build in skills that transfer to less routine work.
3. **AI infrastructure construction is a real, if temporary, counterweight.** AI-related construction hiring currently offsets losses in AI-exposed industries, reinforcing the trades evidence in [[nber-data-centers-local-economies]], with the caveat that construction jobs peak and then fall ([[meta-workforce-academy]]).
4. **Watch layoffs as the signal.** Briggs treats AI-attributed layoffs as the real-time guide. CTE planners tracking AI risk should watch layoff and separation data alongside hiring data.
