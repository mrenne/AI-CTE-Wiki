# No Country for Young Grads (Burning Glass Institute)

**Summary**: A July 2025 Burning Glass Institute report arguing that the bachelor's degree is failing to deliver access to professional work for young graduates, driven by four interlocking forces — AI's "Expertise Upheaval," post-pandemic lean staffing, employer risk aversion, and a growing "Graduate Glut." An early, descriptive framing piece that predates most of the wiki's causal labor-market evidence.

**Sources**: `raw/PDF/No+Country+for+Young+Grads+V_Final7.29.25+(1).pdf`

**Last updated**: 2026-09-23

---

## About this source

Burning Glass Institute report (July 2025) by Gad Levanon, Matt Sigelman, Mariano Mamertino, Mels de Zeeuw, and Gwynn Guilford. It draws on BLS unemployment and layoff data, Lightcast job-postings data, and Felten et al. (2023) LLM exposure scores. It is **descriptive, not causal** — it documents trends and proposes explanations, several of which it labels hypotheses (source: No+Country+for+Young+Grads+V_Final7.29.25+(1).pdf).

Dated July 2025, it is **older than most labor-market sources in the wiki** — it predates the first Canaries paper (August 2025) and the causal work that followed ([[canaries-coal-mine-brynjolfsson]], [[broken-ladder]], [[graduating-into-disruption-census]]). Its value is as an early articulation of frameworks that later studies tested.

---

## The core paradox

White-collar sectors (tech, finance, professional services) posted strong revenues while employment stalled: GDP kept rising while employment in information, finance, insurance, and professional services flattened. Non-college workers and older graduates held steady; **young degree-holders alone saw prospects deteriorate** (source: No+Country+for+Young+Grads+V_Final7.29.25+(1).pdf).

Key figures (source: No+Country+for+Young+Grads+V_Final7.29.25+(1).pdf):
- Unemployment for 20–24-year-olds with at least a bachelor's rose from **5.2% (2018–19) to 6.2%** (two years through June 2025) — now higher than every other education group except high-school-only.
- In 2023 and 2024, the unemployment gap between young graduates and less-educated peers was the **narrowest in three decades**.
- The sharpest unemployment increases for 22–27-year-old grads were in computer occupations (1.7% → 3.5%), management, and business/financial operations.
- Layoff rates for young degree-holders nearly **doubled** from pre-pandemic levels, concentrated in information, finance, and professional services.
- **52% of the Class of 2023** were underemployed (in jobs not requiring a degree) one year after graduation; even in engineering, more than a quarter were underemployed.

The report's chart comparing 20–24-year-olds by attainment includes a separate category for **associate degrees from occupational programs**, but exact values are not recoverable from the PDF text; the authors' text says unemployment for less-educated groups has "generally declined since 2016–2017" (source: No+Country+for+Young+Grads+V_Final7.29.25+(1).pdf).

## The four interlocking forces

### 1. The "Expertise Upheaval" (AI)

Developed with Harvard's Joseph Fuller and Michael Fenlon, the framework distinguishes (source: No+Country+for+Young+Grads+V_Final7.29.25+(1).pdf):

- **Growth Roles** — e.g., marketing specialist, project manager, financial analyst. Learning curves steepen with seniority, and junior work (research, first drafts, basic analysis, routine communication) is exactly what GenAI does well. AI reduces demand for entry-level workers and raises demand for experienced ones, producing a **"Flipped Pyramid"**: the broad junior base through which people used to learn is dismantled. These roles are more likely to require a degree and pay more.
- **Mastery Roles** — roles where AI may *increase* entry-level opportunity by letting people without prior expertise perform at baseline competency.

Supporting evidence: many of the skills most requested in entry-level postings for college grads (customer service, writing and editing, office technology, business operations, project management) have high GenAI exposure. Demand for workers with fewer than 3 years' experience fell sharply in computer, business, and legal occupations while demand for 6+ years held or grew, and the decline in entry-level postings is concentrated almost entirely in medium- and high-LLM-exposure roles — low-exposure junior postings have grown since 2022 (source: No+Country+for+Young+Grads+V_Final7.29.25+(1).pdf).

> "Companies still need human expertise. They just don't have as much need for the entry-level roles where people have traditionally developed that expertise." (source: No+Country+for+Young+Grads+V_Final7.29.25+(1).pdf)

### 2. Lean growth: the "Newfound Efficiency" hypothesis

Pandemic-era skeleton crews pushed firms into automation and process changes; knowledge-economy sectors may have settled into a **lower-employment, higher-productivity equilibrium** where revenue grows without headcount. Job postings in these sectors remain above pre-pandemic levels, but employment relative to output is down (source: No+Country+for+Young+Grads+V_Final7.29.25+(1).pdf).

### 3. "Once Burned, Twice Shy": risk aversion after the Great Resignation

Anecdotal reports and employer surveys suggest managers now see junior hires as risks rather than investments. The authors flag this as hard to measure and possibly temporary (source: No+Country+for+Young+Grads+V_Final7.29.25+(1).pdf).

### 4. The "Graduate Glut"

Most high-school graduates now enroll in college, and the college-educated working-age population is projected to grow by **7–11 million by 2034** even as the total working-age population stagnates. The authors note this trend predates both the pandemic and GenAI. They frame the problem as **underutilization, not oversupply**: "The problem isn't that we're educating too many people; it's that we're wasting their potential" (source: No+Country+for+Young+Grads+V_Final7.29.25+(1).pdf).

**AI as accelerant**: the report's through-line is that AI arrived just as firms were already seeking leaner teams and cutting training investment, providing "the technical means to realize the organizational changes companies were already pursuing" (source: No+Country+for+Young+Grads+V_Final7.29.25+(1).pdf).

## ⚠ Contradiction with later causal evidence

**On the Graduate Glut.** This report treats rising graduate supply as a structural driver of worsening outcomes. [[graduating-into-disruption-census]] (September 2026) tests an oversupply explanation for the most AI-exposed majors and finds it does **not** fit: top-decile graduate supply grew 24% from 2017 to 2022 but was fully absorbed, and employment broke trend only after ChatGPT, when supply growth had already slowed (source: Gradiating into Disruption.pdf).

The two claims differ in scope — Burning Glass describes all young graduates over a decade-long horizon, while the Census paper tests the most-exposed decile of majors around 2022 — so they are not strictly incompatible. But for the fields where AI effects are largest, the more recent causal evidence does not support supply as the explanation. This page does not resolve the difference.

**On attribution.** Burning Glass attributes a share of the junior-hiring decline to pandemic-era lean staffing and risk aversion. [[broken-ladder]] (May 2026) later argued that work-from-home — another pandemic-era shift — was the stronger predictor. Subsequent tests in ADP data ([[canaries-coal-mine-brynjolfsson]]) and Census data ([[graduating-into-disruption-census]]) found the AI effect survives WFH controls. Burning Glass's "multiplicative" framing — several forces reinforcing each other — remains compatible with all of these.

## What this adds to the wiki

- **The Growth Role / Mastery Role distinction** is a useful complement to the BCG segments in [[ai-labor-disruption-segments]]: it asks not whether a job survives AI, but whether AI closes or opens the *entry point* into it.
- **The "Flipped Pyramid"** names the same mechanism the Census paper calls the loss of entry-level work's training function, and that [[canaries-coal-mine-brynjolfsson]] frames as codified vs. tacit knowledge.
- **Underemployment as the outcome to watch**, rather than unemployment — anticipating the Census paper's finding that exposed graduates adjust by sorting into lower-paying sectors.

## Implications for CTE

1. **The college-for-all default is losing its labor-market guarantee.** When over half of recent graduates are underemployed and the degree premium in unemployment is at a 30-year low, CTE pathways — including occupational associate degrees and credentials leading directly to skilled work — deserve reconsideration as primary routes, not fallbacks.
2. **Mastery Roles may be a CTE opportunity.** If AI lowers the expertise barrier to baseline competency in some roles, CTE programs that pair AI fluency with occupational judgment ([[applied-co-intelligence-model]]) are well placed to prepare students for those entry points.
3. **If firms stop training juniors, someone else has to.** The Flipped Pyramid shifts the burden of early skill development away from employers. Work-based learning, apprenticeships, and employer-embedded CTE ([[meta-workforce-academy]], [[berwick-employers-ai-education-gap]]) become more important as a replacement for the disappearing junior rung.
