# Relational Economy

**Summary**: The economic theory that AI-driven automation will not eliminate work but relocate scarcity — shifting employment and expenditure from commodity production into a "relational sector" where human involvement is inseparable from the value of the good or service.

**Sources**: `raw/What will be scarce_.md`, `raw/Why the A.I. Job Apocalypse (Probably) Won't Happen.md`, `raw/DATA CENTERS AND LOCAL ECONOMIES IN THE AGE OF AI.pdf`

**Last updated**: 2026-05-18

---

## The Central Question

Economics is the study of scarcity. If AI makes cognitive and informational production cheap — writing briefs, generating images, diagnosing from scans — what becomes scarce next? This is the framing Alex Imas (University of Chicago) uses to challenge the dominant AI-labor narrative:

> "The answer to any question about the future economics of advanced AI begins with identifying what becomes scarce." (source: What will be scarce_.md)

The relational economy thesis holds that human labor, attention, and provenance become scarce — and therefore more economically valuable — precisely because AI commodifies everything else.

## Structural Change: The Historical Precedent

Economies routinely shift what is abundant and what is scarce. The canonical example is agriculture: in 1900, 40% of the American workforce farmed. Today it's under 2%. Agricultural output boomed; prices fell; but the sector shrank as a share of GDP and employment shifted to manufacturing and then services.

The economic mechanism is **nonhomothetic demand** (Comin, Lashkari & Mestieri, *Econometrica* 2021): as people get richer, they don't buy proportionally more of everything — they shift spending toward sectors with higher income elasticity. Agriculture has low income elasticity (you can only eat so much). Services have high income elasticity (there is always a better restaurant, a more engaging experience, a more attentive doctor).

The key quantitative finding: **income effects account for over 75% of observed patterns of structural change**. Price effects — the standard story that automated sectors get cheap so people buy other stuff — account for only about a quarter. The dominant force is simpler: as societies get richer, they want fundamentally different things. (source: What will be scarce_.md)

If AI makes cognitive commodity production cheap, the same logic predicts: spending and employment shift toward sectors with higher income elasticity. The question is which sectors those are.

## The Relational Sector

Imas's answer is the **relational sector**: goods and services whose value is inseparable from the human who provides them. This includes:

- **Care work**: nurses, therapists, childcare workers, personal trainers
- **Hospitality**: boutique hotels, restaurants, guides, personal chefs
- **Education**: tutors who know the student, mentors, coaches
- **Artisanal production**: bespoke tailors, craft brewers, handmade art, provenance-rich food
- **Performance and community**: live performers, spiritual guides, community curators
- **Emerging roles**: experience designers, provenance certifiers, human-AI collaboration artists

The defining characteristic: the good or service cannot be fully commodified because **the human's involvement is part of what the consumer is purchasing**. An AI-written brief may be functionally identical to a human-written one. A therapy session from a therapist who knows your history is not interchangeable with an AI conversation, and consumers know it.

The **Starbucks example** illustrates the dynamic at scale: the company spent years automating coffee production to cut costs. CEO Brian Niccol reversed course, hiring more baristas per store, bringing back ceramic cups and handwritten notes. Conclusion: "small details and hospitality drive satisfaction." The automation reduced the experience; the experience was the product. (source: What will be scarce_.md)

## Mimetic Desire: The Behavioral Foundation

Why does the relational sector have especially high income elasticity — higher than ordinary services? Imas grounds the answer in **mimetic desire** (René Girard): people don't desire objects only for their intrinsic properties, but because other people desire them and can't have them. Desire is comparative, status-laden, and never satiated.

This runs through centuries of thought:
- Augustine's *libido dominandi* — the desire for possession that others are denied
- Hobbes's *Leviathan* — competition for "eminence" over others, never satisfied
- Rousseau's *amour propre* — joy from feeling superior, pain from feeling inferior, ratcheting upward without limit

The economic implication: goods with mimetic properties have **inherently high income elasticity** because the comparative dimension of desire does not saturate. The more others have commodity goods, the more valuable the non-commodity version becomes.

Imas and Madarasz provide experimental evidence (source: What will be scarce_.md):
- Willingness to pay **roughly doubled** when subjects learned a random subset of buyers would be excluded from a product — even when the product was identical and exclusion was random (not a quality signal)
- The demand curve shifted substantially rightward as the prospect of exclusion increased

New work with Mandel finds that **AI involvement specifically undermines the exclusivity premium**: human-made artwork gained 44% in value from being one-of-a-kind; AI-generated artwork gained only 21%. "The mere involvement of AI made the work feel inherently non-exclusive, as if it could always be reproduced." (source: What will be scarce_.md)

This implies that as AI produces more commodity-quality content, the premium on provably human-made goods and services rises — a structural reinforcement of the relational sector's income elasticity.

## Baumol's Cost Disease as a Feature

The relational sector is characterized by what economists call **Baumol's cost disease**: productivity improvements are difficult because the human element is the product. A therapist cannot serve twice as many clients per hour by automating. A personal trainer cannot scale like a software product.

In the standard economic critique, Baumol's cost disease is a problem: the stagnant sector gets relatively expensive and crowds out more productive uses of income. But in the relational economy thesis, it is a **feature**:

- The relational sector remains expensive relative to commoditized alternatives
- Relative expensiveness sustains high wages for relational workers
- Rising commodity abundance means a growing share of income can flow to the relational sector
- The sector that resists automation is where employment grows

> "The relative expense of human services stops being a budget problem and starts being a labor market solution. The 'stagnant' sector, the one that resists automation, is precisely where spending and employment grow." (source: What will be scarce_.md)

## The Jevons Paradox Connection

A parallel mechanism operates through **latent demand release** — what Ezra Klein calls the Jevons Paradox applied to AI (source: Why the A.I. Job Apocalypse (Probably) Won't Happen.md):

When VisiCalc (the first electronic spreadsheet) was released in 1979, it could do in minutes what teams of accountants took days to do. Predictions of mass unemployment for bookkeepers followed. Instead, the number of accountants quadrupled over the next 40 years. The spreadsheet didn't replace the accountant; it "unleashed latent demand for financial intelligence that had been there all along, waiting for costs to fall far enough to be satisfied." (Eldar Maksymov, ASU)

Jevons originally observed this with coal: Watt's more efficient steam engine didn't reduce coal use; it made coal cheap enough to use for more things, tripling demand. The same pattern has repeated across major technological transitions: "In every major occupational group that adopted computers heavily, employment grew faster than in groups that did not." (source: Why the A.I. Job Apocalypse (Probably) Won't Happen.md)

Applied to AI: if the relational sector has enormous latent demand that was previously priced out, AI-driven cost reductions in commodity sectors may release purchasing power that flows toward more human contact, not less.

## The Structural Change Prediction

The formal model generates a clean, testable prediction (source: What will be scarce_.md):

1. AI automates commodity production → prices in that sector fall
2. Falling prices raise real income
3. Rising real income shifts demand toward high-income-elasticity sectors
4. The relational sector, with its mimetic and experiential properties, has especially high income elasticity
5. Baumol's cost disease keeps the relational sector expensive relative to commodities
6. Therefore: **automated sectors shrink as shares of GDP; relational sectors grow**

This mirrors the historical farm-to-factory-to-services transition, but the next destination is the relational sector rather than generic services.

Important caveats from Imas (source: What will be scarce_.md):
- This argument is strongest for **rich countries** where rising incomes can fund the transition; developing economies built on commodity production for wealthy nations face a more complicated picture
- Labor's aggregate share of income may still fall even as the relational sector grows — the claim is about sectoral reallocation, not labor's total share
- The model says labor remains a *substantial* part of the economy, not necessarily that it grows as a share

## The Counterargument: Skills Atrophy

Klein raises a critical challenge to the relational economy thesis: **relational skills may be deteriorating at the same moment they become most economically valuable** (source: Why the A.I. Job Apocalypse (Probably) Won't Happen.md):

- High school seniors spending time with friends: ~12 hours/week in 2003 → ~5 hours/week in 2024
- High school seniors who report going on a date: 80% in 2000 → 46% in 2024
- Gen Z reporting no sex in the past year: ~25%

If AI is accelerating this social withdrawal by offering a digital simulacrum of relationships — responsive, frictionless, never demanding — it may be degrading the capacity for the deep human connection that the relational economy requires. See also [[cognitive-debt]] for the parallel argument about how AI use degrades cognitive capability before it becomes economically necessary.

> "If Imas is right — and I think he is — our ability to relate, sensitively and deeply, to other human beings will be a central and valuable skill. That, I fear, is the exact skill we are breaking down in the young." (source: Why the A.I. Job Apocalypse (Probably) Won't Happen.md)

## Implications for CTE and Education

The relational economy thesis has direct implications for [[cte-and-ai]]:

1. **CTE pathways are largely relational pathways.** Healthcare, hospitality, early childhood education, cosmetology, culinary arts, construction, personal services — these are precisely the sectors Imas identifies as the relational sector's core. If the thesis is correct, CTE is not a second-tier path into a vulnerable labor market; it is preparation for the sectors that absorb employment as commoditization advances.

2. **The framing shifts from defensive to affirmative.** CTE has often been framed as protection against automation (physical work is hard to automate). The relational economy thesis offers a stronger framing: CTE develops the human capacities — warmth, judgment, presence, skilled attention — that *are the product* in the growing sector of the economy.

3. **Relational skills need deliberate development.** If [[transferable-skills]] — empathy, communication, collaborative problem-solving, emotional regulation — are becoming economically central, then programs that develop them are building the labor market's most durable assets.

4. **AI may help the relational sector, not threaten it.** Klein's personal observation: the better his AI got, the more he wanted from the humans in his life. AI as a complement to human relationships rather than a substitute for them is the optimistic version of the relational economy — and it is consistent with the Jevons Paradox logic.

## The Infrastructure Economy as Relational Economy Complement

The NBER data centers paper ([[nber-data-centers-local-economies]]) adds an empirical dimension to the relational economy thesis: the physical infrastructure enabling AI is itself a site of human labor that is resistant to automation. The IV evidence shows that data center construction creates durable local employment concentrated in trades — electricians, HVAC, plumbers — precisely the CTE-pipeline occupations with high physical presence requirements and contextual judgment demands.

This reinforces the relational economy framework from a different angle: the relational sector isn't the only protected zone. The physical layer of the AI economy — installing and maintaining the infrastructure that makes cognitive commodity production possible — also demands human presence and skill. The commodity economy does not float free of physical work; it depends on it. (source: DATA CENTERS AND LOCAL ECONOMIES IN THE AGE OF AI.pdf)
