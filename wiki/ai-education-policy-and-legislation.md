# AI Education Policy and Legislation

**Summary**: A concept page tracking concrete governmental and legislative instruments — state-level guidance frameworks, procurement standards, and proposed federal/state legislation — addressing AI in K-12 education, as distinct from the wiki's research findings and practitioner accounts.

**Sources**: `raw/misc/Senate Hearing on Artificial Intelligence and K-12 Education.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-07-08

## Why This Page Exists

Most of the wiki's evidence base comes from academic research, journalism, and practitioner/webinar accounts. The June 2026 Senate HELP subcommittee hearing on AI and K-12 education ([[senate-hearing-ai-k12-education]]) is the wiki's first source describing actual governmental policy instruments in operation or under consideration — state guidance frameworks, procurement standards, and pending legislation. This page collects those instruments so future policy-focused sources have a natural home, consistent with the wiki's practice of building dedicated concept pages once a topic accumulates enough independent material (see [[ai-occupational-exposure-index]] for the same pattern applied to a shared research methodology).

## State-Level Instruments

### Delaware: AI Assurance Lab

An independent, pre-adoption testing process for AI education tools, established by the Delaware Department of Education under Secretary Cindy Marten. Rather than relying on vendor claims, trained educators, administrators, technical experts, and DOE staff test tools in real classroom scenarios against national frameworks and the state's own guidance — evaluating accuracy, bias, appropriateness, and accessibility. Delaware's 2024 statewide generative-AI guidance rests on four priorities: protect student data, ensure ethical use, promote equity, and enhance teaching and learning. The state's strategic-plan target is 75% of districts and charter schools using AI tools while maintaining full compliance with data security, privacy, and human-in-the-loop requirements. The "Empowering Leaders Toolkit" operationalizes this as a three-step roadmap: mitigate risk → build strategy around the instructional core → guide and evaluate use over time. (source: Senate Hearing on Artificial Intelligence and K-12 Education.md)

### Alabama: Data Scholars Program

A state-level workforce pipeline built through a QuantHub/Alabama Department of Education partnership: paid AI/data-science internships (100+ placements across 50+ employers), industry-recognized credentials earned before high school graduation, and a curriculum-generation process explicitly redesigned around AI's pace of change (semester-length refresh cycles rather than the traditional five-to-ten-year textbook cycle). See [[senate-hearing-ai-k12-education]] for the full case study and [[cte-and-ai]] for its CTE relevance.

### Florida: AI Civil Rights Bill

Establishes "consequential decision-making" requirements — a mandatory human reviewer whenever AI contributes to decisions such as loan approval, college admission, financial aid, or insurance claims. Cited in the hearing as a model for keeping "a human in the loop" on high-stakes automated decisions affecting students and families, extending beyond the classroom into the broader civil-rights implications of AI systems that touch education-adjacent decisions.

## Federal-Level Instruments and Proposals

### The RAYS Act

A bipartisan bill (referenced by Sens. Blunt Rochester and Husted as co-sponsors) that would require *states* — not the federal government — to establish statewide standards for AI use in K-12 classrooms. Reflects a consistent theme across the hearing's witnesses: federal guidance and evidence-building should support state and local decision-making, not supplant it.

### Existing Federal Funding Mechanisms Repeatedly Cited as the Practical Levers

- **Title II and Title IV**: professional-development funding named by multiple witnesses as critical to AI-literacy training for educators — "fully funding Title II and Title IV dollars is critical" (Mote).
- **E-Rate**: internet connectivity funding for rural/under-resourced districts, flagged as chronically oversubscribed relative to need.
- **Perkins V / CLNA**: not raised directly in this hearing, but consistent with [[cte-futures-aci-webinar]]'s argument that the existing Comprehensive Local Needs Assessment mechanism, not new standalone AI legislation, is the practical implementation vehicle for continuous CTE curriculum alignment with AI-driven labor market change.

### Proposed Evaluation and Procurement Frameworks (Not Yet Codified)

Erin Mote proposed frameworks that are not law but represent a policy-advocacy position with potential to inform future legislation or state guidance:

- **Five Tech Quality Indicators**: safe, interoperable, usable, inclusive, evidence-based — proposed as a baseline procurement standard.
- **"Waterfall" regulatory approach**: heaviest safety controls on highest-risk consumer/companion AI platforms; lighter burden on purpose-built, lower-risk educational tools — an explicit alternative to blanket bans or uniform screen-time limits.
- **"Return on Instruction"** as a replacement outcome metric for simple engagement/usage tracking.

## A Consistent Cross-Witness Theme: State Authority, Federal Support

Despite differing institutional vantage points (a state secretary of education, an education-technology entrepreneur, and a nonprofit/advocacy CEO), all three witnesses converged on the same federalism position: states and districts should retain decision-making authority over specific AI tool adoption and classroom implementation, while the federal government's role is to fund research, build shared evidence infrastructure, support professional development, and set baseline safety/privacy floors — not to mandate specific tools or curricula. This is a notable point of consensus in an otherwise polarized policy hearing (the hearing opened with a sharp partisan exchange over the Department of Education's restructuring).

## Limitations

This page is built entirely from a single congressional hearing's testimony, which is advocacy-inflected by design (witnesses are there to make a case, not to present neutral synthesis) and transcribed via imperfect auto-captioning (see the source-quality caveat in [[senate-hearing-ai-k12-education]]). Numeric claims cited in testimony without an academic source (e.g., the 4.5pp/10pp learning-gains figures, the screen-reading-effectiveness claim) are flagged as needing independent verification, not treated as established findings. This page should be expanded as additional policy-focused sources are ingested.
