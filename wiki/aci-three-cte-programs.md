# Applied Co-Intelligence: How Three CTE Programs Keep Humans at the Center of AI

**Summary**: A CTE Futures blog post (August 2026) presenting the first real-world implementation case studies of the Applied Co-Intelligence (ACI) model — Walters State Community College (agriculture), TCAP Upper Cumberland (welding), and Jackson State Community College (a three-program institutional rollout) — showing how the ACI framework translates into classroom practice at three different scales of AI maturity.

**Sources**: `raw/blog/Applied Co-Intelligence_ How Three CTE Programs Keep Humans at the Center of AI.md` (Cameron Sublett, CTE Futures, August 27, 2026)

**Last updated**: 2026-08-28

---

## About this source

The third public document from the CTE Futures team on the Applied Co-Intelligence model, following the original [[applied-co-intelligence-report]] and the [[cte-futures-aci-webinar]]. Where the report proposed the ACI model theoretically and the webinar discussed implementation challenges in general terms, this piece is the first to document **specific, named programs actually implementing it**, with outcomes. All three examples are Tennessee institutions (consistent with the CTE Futures team's TCAT/Tennessee-community-college network already documented via [[fostering-ai-ready-building-moving]] and related sources).

## Case 1: Walters State Community College — Agriculture Business

**Implementation**: In an AAS Business/Agriculture Business program, working with USDA and county soil-science workforce partners, students used ChatGPT/Copilot to answer advanced lecture questions that couldn't be answered directly from the textbook — forcing evaluation of AI output against domain knowledge, not just retrieval. The signature assignment: a scenario asking students to identify soil-profile characteristics during a septic-system site evaluation *and* explain a permit denial to a homeowner with empathy — an explicit pairing of technical skill (soil science) and transferable skill (communicating a difficult technical judgment).

**Outcome**: The spring cohort that used AI showed significantly higher AI-literacy gains than a fall cohort that didn't, *and* achieved similarly strong course-learning-outcome gains — i.e., the AI-integrated cohort did not sacrifice subject-matter learning to gain AI literacy. The instructor also measured ethical awareness directly, testing students on algorithmic bias and lack-of-transparency concepts specific to soil science — a rare example in the wiki's evidence base of an AI-ethics assessment embedded in a technical subject rather than taught as a separate module.

## Case 2: TCAP Upper Cumberland — Welding & Industrial Maintenance

**Implementation**: Embedded a Xiris weld camera system with AI-driven real-time quality feedback (identifying weld irregularities as they occur) into hands-on lab instruction, in partnership with Welding Werks, LLC.

**Outcome**: The technical result — students seeing immediate cause-and-effect between welding variables and quality — was less notable than the organizational one: implementation required tight collaboration across welding, industrial maintenance, and IT departments, which had never previously worked together. This produced genuine cross-training (welding students learning robotics/programming; industrial maintenance students learning welding processes) as a side effect of AI adoption, not a designed outcome — an unplanned instance of the ACI model's transferable-skills sphere emerging from technical-skill integration.

## Case 3: Jackson State Community College — Institution-Wide Rollout

**Implementation**: A deliberate three-pronged strategy spanning Computer Information Technology, Engineering Systems Technology, and Health Sciences: (1) faculty professional development with stipends and ongoing consultation, recognizing instructor confidence as the precondition for any classroom AI literacy; (2) occupation-specific AI tools embedded into student projects in each discipline (Raspberry Pi → Dell AI-enabled computers in Engineering Systems; bias-detection exercises on patient datasets in Health Sciences; 3D printing → networking implementations in CIT); (3) a public spring AI Summit where students presented to 200+ educators, employers, and workforce/civic leaders (the Madison County Mayor and Greater Jackson Chamber CEO among them).

**Outcome**: The college's own framing — presenting AI "strictly as a decision-support tool rather than a classroom shortcut," explicitly reinforcing that students "remain the ultimate experts in their respective trades" — is close to a direct restatement of the ACI model's core thesis. The Summit is notable as a public accountability/community-engagement mechanism for AI adoption not previously documented in the wiki's CTE sources: presenting institutional AI work to regional economic and civic leaders, rather than only to educators, as a driver of faculty buy-in.

## What this adds to the wiki's ACI coverage

The wiki previously had the ACI model as theory ([[applied-co-intelligence-model]]) and as a framework discussed by researchers and practitioners in a webinar setting ([[cte-futures-aci-webinar]], plus the Alabama Data Scholars implementation via [[senate-hearing-ai-k12-education]] and [[quanthub-data-scholars-2026-update]]). This piece adds three more, smaller-scale, directly-observed classroom implementations — with a range of AI maturity from simple prompt-based assignments (Walters State) to specialized industrial hardware (TCAP Upper Cumberland) to full institutional programs (Jackson State). Notably, **none of these three examples report a null or negative result** — a pattern worth flagging rather than treating as automatically confirmatory, since the source is itself produced by the CTE Futures team promoting the ACI model they authored, and none of the three case studies used a comparison-group design capable of ruling out selection or Hawthorne effects.

## A Natural Contrast with the Khanmigo RCT

[[one-click-away-khanmigo-rct]] (NBER, August 2026) — an unrelated, methodologically rigorous RCT of AI tutoring — found that student-initiated engagement with a general-purpose, decontextualized AI tutor (Khanmigo, deployed for remedial math) was thin: the median student engaged in only 14% of exercise sessions and sent substantive mathematical messages in only 1 in 7 interactions. The Walters State and TCAP Upper Cumberland cases, by contrast, **built AI engagement into the assessed task itself** — a scenario question that requires evaluating AI output against domain knowledge; a hardware system that surfaces AI feedback automatically during welding rather than requiring a student to seek it out.

This is not a controlled comparison — the two sources measure completely different interventions, populations, and outcomes, and the CTE case studies lack the Khanmigo RCT's rigor. But the contrast is informative: it is consistent with the applied-co-intelligence-model's central design claim that AI integrated into occupational context, with structured tasks forcing engagement, may avoid the student-initiative problem that the NBER study identifies as the binding constraint on general-purpose AI tutoring. Whether this holds under a comparably rigorous evaluation of an ACI-model classroom is an open question the wiki cannot yet answer — no ACI case study to date uses a randomized or quasi-experimental design.

## Implications for CTE

1. **The Walters State finding — AI literacy gains without sacrificing subject-matter learning — is one of the only positive, dual-outcome results in the wiki's CTE evidence base**, and is worth tracking for replication, since most of the wiki's other AI-in-education findings show a tradeoff (see [[one-click-away-khanmigo-rct]], [[hardman-cognitive-offloading-paradox]]) rather than a joint gain.
2. **The Jackson State AI Summit model is a concrete, replicable community-engagement mechanism** other CTE programs could adopt to build faculty buy-in and stakeholder legitimacy for AI integration — distinct from, and complementary to, the CLNA-as-implementation-vehicle policy mechanism already documented in [[cte-futures-aci-webinar]].
3. **The TCAP Upper Cumberland cross-departmental collaboration** is a real-world instance of the "transferable skills" sphere of the ACI model emerging as a *side effect* of technical AI integration rather than being separately taught — a data point for CTE curriculum designers who assume transferable-skill development requires dedicated instructional time.
4. **A caution on evidentiary weight**: this source is the CTE Futures team's own promotional/dissemination content for a model they created, drawing only from case studies without comparison groups. It should be read as implementation documentation, not as evidence the ACI model outperforms alternatives.
