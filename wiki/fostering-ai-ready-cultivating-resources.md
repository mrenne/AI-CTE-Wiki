# Fostering AI-Ready Cultivating Resources CTE Pipelines

**Summary**: ERO/Advance CTE/ACTE report (April 2025) on AI's impact on the Cultivating Resources Career Cluster Grouping — Agriculture and Energy & Natural Resources — finding exposure close to the national average, with bachelor's-level science and management roles as the main outliers in an otherwise low-exposure grouping.

**Sources**: `raw/PDF/Fostering-AI-Ready-Cultivating-Resources-CTE-Pipelines.pdf`

**Last updated**: 2026-07-02

## About this report

By Cameron Sublett, Dani Rimbach-Jones, Lauren Mason, Karina Beltran, & Matias Fresard. Education Research & Opportunity Center (ERO), University of Tennessee Knoxville, in partnership with Advance CTE and ACTE, funded by ECMC Foundation. April 2025. Third or fourth in the four-part series; see [[fostering-ai-ready-building-moving]], [[fostering-ai-ready-caring-communities]], and [[fostering-ai-ready-creating-experiencing]] for the other three, and [[ai-occupational-exposure-index]] for the shared methodology.

## What is the Cultivating Resources cluster grouping?

The Cultivating Resources grouping consists of two Career Clusters:

- **Agriculture** (sub-clusters: Agribusiness, Agricultural Technology & Automation, Animal Systems, Food & Science Processing, Plant Systems, Water Systems)
- **Energy & Natural Resources** (sub-clusters: Clean & Alternative Energy, Conservation & Land Management, Ecological Research & Development, Environmental Protection, Resource Extraction, Utilities)

## How AI is revolutionizing these occupations

**Agriculture**: precision agriculture technologies use computer vision and machine learning for targeted intervention — John Deere's See & Spray Select system identifies and treats individual weeds rather than blanket-spraying fields. Cainthus, an Irish dairy AI startup, uses computer vision for livestock monitoring. Platforms in the Taranis/AeroFarms family apply AI to irrigation and crop-health management.

Dr. Kevin Wade, Director of Dairy Information Systems at McGill University, described how machine learning models trained on video and sensor data can detect lameness in dairy cows earlier and more consistently than routine human observation — improving animal welfare outcomes and reducing the labor burden of manual herd health checks.

**Energy & Natural Resources**: conservation technology applications include AI-powered anti-poaching systems (in the PAWS/SMART family) that predict poacher movement patterns from historical data, and wildlife-monitoring computer vision systems — Norouzzadeh et al. (2018) report 99.3% accuracy identifying species in camera-trap images from the African Serengeti, a task that previously required extensive manual review by researchers and citizen-science volunteers.

Daniel Goldsmith of Julius Education discussed using AI and large language models to identify and classify emerging "green jobs" in labor market data, while noting a structural limitation: existing Standard Occupational Classification (SOC) codes were not designed to capture new energy-transition roles, making it difficult for workforce data systems to track this employment growth using conventional taxonomies.

Dr. Ben Hertz-Shargel, Global Head of Grid Edge at Wood Mackenzie, framed AI's role in the electric grid workforce primarily as a training and upskilling tool rather than a replacement technology — describing AI's value as "removing the manual side" of grid operations analysis so that human operators can be upskilled into higher-judgment supervisory and diagnostic roles rather than being displaced by automation.

## Will AI automate Cultivating Resources occupations?

Applying the [[ai-occupational-exposure-index]] methodology, both Agriculture and Energy & Natural Resources cluster AIOE averages sit **close to the national average** — Agriculture slightly below, Energy & Natural Resources slightly above — placing this grouping in the middle of the four studied so far, more exposed than Building & Moving but less exposed than Caring for Communities.

As in the other three reports, the exceptions are concentrated in bachelor's-degree-requiring science and management occupations: **Natural sciences managers (AIOE 1.33), Biological scientists (AIOE 1.01), and Environmental scientists (AIOE 0.93)** score well above the national mean, driven by cognitive-task intensity (data analysis, research, regulatory compliance). By contrast, hands-on field and production roles score low — **Farmworkers and laborers (AIOE −1.6)** among the most insulated occupations across the entire four-report series. This is the same education-stratification pattern documented in every other report in the series (source: Fostering-AI-Ready-Cultivating-Resources-CTE-Pipelines.pdf, citing Felten et al., 2021).

## Recommendations

The report follows the same four-audience structure as the other reports in the series:

- **CTE providers**: shift AI training from optional to mandatory across Agriculture and Energy & Natural Resources programs; address the equity gap in AI training access between affluent and open-access institutions (Palmer, 2025).
- **Focus on skills, not only jobs**: technology acts on tasks, not occupations directly; Agriculture and Energy programs should identify which specific tasks (irrigation scheduling, herd monitoring, grid diagnostics) are shifting toward AI-assisted execution and adjust curricula accordingly.
- **Industry partners**: share AI use cases and applications specific to regional agricultural and energy economies, which vary considerably by geography (row-crop agriculture vs. livestock vs. grid infrastructure).
- **Policymakers**: leverage the Perkins V Comprehensive Local Needs Assessment; continue funding for apprenticeships and equipment access, particularly given the capital intensity of precision-agriculture and grid-monitoring technology.

## Conclusion

As in the other three reports, the authors conclude that AI is unlikely to fully automate Agriculture or Energy & Natural Resources occupations. Instead, AI is expected to concentrate in decision-support and monitoring roles — helping human workers detect problems earlier (lameness in livestock, poachers approaching a reserve, grid anomalies) — while the underlying physical, judgment-intensive work of farming, land management, and infrastructure maintenance remains human-centered.
