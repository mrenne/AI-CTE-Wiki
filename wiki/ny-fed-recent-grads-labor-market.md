# NY Fed: The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates

**Summary**: The Federal Reserve Bank of New York's quarterly tracker of employment outcomes for recent college graduates (ages 22–27, bachelor's degree or higher). In 2026:Q3 recent-graduate unemployment fell to 5.2%, its lowest in more than a year, while underemployment rose to 42.5%. The series covers bachelor's holders only, so it says nothing about associate-degree or certificate completers.

**Sources**: `raw/misc/The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-11-23

## About this source

A clipping, made November 23, 2026, of the New York Fed's "Labor Market for Recent College Graduates" web feature (source: The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates.md). The page is built around interactive charts, but the chart data did not survive the clipping. What the wiki has is the 2026:Q3 highlights paragraph and the page's methodology FAQ. Unemployment by major, wages, and the historical series are not in the clipping.

The NY Fed notes that the figures "do not represent official estimates" of the Bank or the Federal Reserve System (source: The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates.md).

## The 2026:Q3 figures

"Labor market conditions improved for recent college graduates in the third quarter of 2026 but remained challenging" (source: The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates.md):

- **Unemployment fell to 5.2%**, its lowest level in more than a year. The Q2 figure, cited in the wiki via Forbes, was 5.6% ([[safest-college-degrees-forbes]]).
- **Underemployment ticked up to 42.5%**: the share of recent graduates working in jobs that typically do not require a college degree.

The two measures moved in opposite directions: fewer recent graduates were jobless, but more of those working were in jobs that don't need their degree.

## How the series is built

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Who counts as a recent graduate | Ages 22–27 ("early career"); mid-career is 35–45 |
| Education covered | Bachelor's degree or higher, including graduate and professional degrees. **No data on associate degrees or technical certificates** |
| Geography | National |
| Time span | 1990 to present |
| Update schedule | Unemployment and underemployment quarterly (usually February, May, August, November); wages and outcomes by major annually (February) |
| Underemployment definition | Working in a job that typically does not require a bachelor's degree, judged from O\*NET Education and Training Questionnaire data |
| Outcomes by major | Most recent year of American Community Survey data only; no historical by-major series |
| Discontinued | Since 2023:Q3 the feature no longer reports which kinds of non-degree jobs underemployed graduates hold ("good non-college jobs" vs. "low-wage jobs"); historical data remain downloadable |

(source: The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates.md)

The framework comes from two earlier studies, "Underemployment in the Early Careers of College Graduates following the Great Recession" (NBER) and "Are Recent College Graduates Finding Good Jobs?" (NY Fed, 2014) (source: The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates.md).

## Implications for CTE

These are the wiki's inferences, not claims made by the source.

1. **Watch underemployment, not just unemployment.** For students weighing a four-year degree, more than two in five recent graduates are in jobs that don't need one. That is the relevant comparison for CTE pathways that lead directly to skilled work.
2. **CTE completers are invisible in this benchmark.** Public debate about "how new graduates are doing" draws on bachelor's-only data. CTE programs need their own outcome data to be part of that conversation ([[education-to-workforce-data-systems]]).
3. **Quarterly updates make this a useful tracking source.** The next releases (February, May 2027) will show whether the Q3 improvement holds.
