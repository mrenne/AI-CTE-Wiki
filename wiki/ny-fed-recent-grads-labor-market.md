# NY Fed: The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates

**Summary**: The Federal Reserve Bank of New York's quarterly tracker of recent college graduates (ages 22–27, bachelor's or higher) and its underlying data file: in 2026:Q3 unemployment fell to 5.2% while underemployment rose to 42.5%. The data, which run back to 1990, show recent graduates' unemployment advantage over young workers at its smallest on record, flat real pay, and computer science and computer engineering combining some of the highest unemployment with the highest starting pay.

**Sources**: `raw/misc/The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates.md`, `raw/misc/College-labor-data.xlsx`

**Last updated**: 2026-11-23

---

## About this source

A clipping, made November 23, 2026, of the New York Fed's "Labor Market for Recent College Graduates" web feature (source: The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates.md). The page is built around interactive charts, whose data did not survive the clipping. The user then supplied the NY Fed's downloadable data file (source: College-labor-data.xlsx), which holds the series behind the charts: monthly unemployment and underemployment from January 1990 to September 2026 (updated November 2, 2026), annual wages 1990–2025, and outcomes for 73 majors (both updated February 4, 2026). The file's September 2026 values (5.23% unemployment, 42.46% underemployment) match the article's Q3 figures (source: College-labor-data.xlsx). Calculations on this page from the file (annual averages, gaps, ranks) are the wiki's own.

The NY Fed notes that the figures "do not represent official estimates" of the Bank or the Federal Reserve System (source: The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates.md).

## The 2026:Q3 figures

"Labor market conditions improved for recent college graduates in the third quarter of 2026 but remained challenging" (source: The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates.md):

- **Unemployment fell to 5.2%**, its lowest level in more than a year. The Q2 figure, cited in the wiki via Forbes, was 5.6% ([[safest-college-degrees-forbes]]).
- **Underemployment ticked up to 42.5%**: the share of recent graduates working in jobs that typically do not require a college degree.

The two measures moved in opposite directions: fewer recent graduates were jobless, but more of those working were in jobs that don't need their degree.

## How the series is built

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Who counts as a recent graduate | Ages 22–27 ("early career"); mid-career is 35–45 |
| Education covered | Bachelor's degree or higher, including graduate and professional degrees. **No data on associate degrees or technical certificates** |
| Geography | National |
| Time span | 1990 to present |
| Update schedule | Unemployment and underemployment quarterly (usually February, May, August, November); wages and outcomes by major annually (February) |
| Underemployment definition | Working in a job that typically does not require a bachelor's degree, judged from O\*NET Education and Training Questionnaire data |
| Outcomes by major | Most recent year of American Community Survey data only; no historical by-major series |
| Discontinued | Since 2023:Q3 the feature no longer reports which kinds of non-degree jobs underemployed graduates hold ("good non-college jobs" vs. "low-wage jobs"); historical data remain downloadable |

(source: The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates.md)

The framework comes from two earlier studies, "Underemployment in the Early Careers of College Graduates following the Great Recession" (NBER) and "Are Recent College Graduates Finding Good Jobs?" (NY Fed, 2014) (source: The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates.md).

## What the data file shows

### 1. The degree's unemployment advantage is the smallest on record (same-age comparison)

The file compares recent graduates with "young workers" and with "all workers." The file does not define "young workers"; the feature's FAQ uses 22–27 for early-career graduates, so the young-worker series is presumably the same age band at all education levels (source: College-labor-data.xlsx; The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates.md). Annual averages computed from the monthly data:

| Year | Recent graduates | Young workers | All workers | Advantage over young workers (points) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1990 | 3.6% | 7.9% | 5.3% | 4.3 |
| 2000 | 2.8% | 6.3% | 3.8% | 3.5 |
| 2010 | 7.0% | 15.5% | 9.5% | 8.5 |
| 2019 | 3.9% | 6.5% | 3.6% | 2.6 |
| 2024 | 4.8% | 6.6% | 3.9% | 1.8 |
| 2026 (Jan–Sep) | 5.5% | 7.3% | 4.1% | 1.75 |

(source: College-labor-data.xlsx)

- **Against young workers**, recent graduates still have lower unemployment, but the gap in 2026 is the smallest annual gap in the 36-year series. This independently confirms Burning Glass's finding that the degree's unemployment advantage is at a 30-year low ([[no-country-young-grads-burning-glass]]).
- **Against all workers** (every age), recent graduates have had *higher* unemployment every month since January 2021, after being lower for most of 1990–2018. The 2026 average gap (+1.4 points) is the largest in the series. This comparison mixes ages, so it overstates the problem; the same-age comparison above is the fairer test.

### 2. Underemployment is rising fast, but is not at a record

Recent-graduate underemployment fell to 38.6% in December 2024, then rose to 42.5% by September 2026, the highest since October 2020. It is still below the 2005–2015 level (about 45%) and the series peak of 48.4% (November 1992). Underemployment among all college graduates has stayed near 33–35% throughout (source: College-labor-data.xlsx).

### 3. Real pay has barely moved since 1990

| Year | Recent-graduate median | 25th percentile | 75th percentile | High-school diploma median | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1990 | $58,138 | $44,357 | $73,929 | $41,893 | 1.39 |
| 2000 | $56,097 | $41,138 | $74,796 | $37,959 | 1.48 |
| 2015 | $59,776 | $43,474 | $81,513 | $35,322 | 1.69 |
| 2019 | $56,675 | $40,302 | $81,864 | $37,783 | 1.50 |
| 2025 | $60,000 | $45,000 | $85,000 | $40,000 | 1.50 |

(source: College-labor-data.xlsx)

The file lists the Consumer Price Index among the wage sources, indicating the figures are inflation-adjusted (source: College-labor-data.xlsx). The degree still pays about 50% more than a high-school diploma, but the median graduate's real pay is about where it was in 1990; only the 75th percentile has risen noticeably.

### 4. Outcomes by major: the computing paradox

Across 73 majors (overall: 4.2% unemployment, 39.4% underemployment, $58,000 early-career median) (source: College-labor-data.xlsx):

| Major | Unemployment (rank of 73) | Underemployment | Early-career median | Mid-career median |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Computer Engineering | 7.8% (2nd) | 15.8% | $90,000 | $131,000 |
| Computer Science | 7.0% (4th) | 19.1% | $87,000 | $120,000 |
| Information Systems & Management | 6.0% (14th) | 25.6% | $67,000 | $100,000 |
| Nursing | 2.1% | 12.8% (lowest) | $70,000 | $87,000 |
| Construction Services | 2.2% | 17.9% | $75,000 | $120,000 |
| Engineering Technologies | 1.7% | 44.4% | $65,000 | $104,000 |
| Medical Technicians | 6.2% (11th) | 47.0% | $62,000 | $80,000 |
| Criminal Justice | 3.6% | 65.8% (highest) | $50,000 | $80,000 |

(source: College-labor-data.xlsx)

**The computing paradox.** Computer engineering and computer science combine some of the highest unemployment with some of the lowest underemployment and the highest starting pay. Graduates in these fields tend either to land a well-paid degree-level job or to have no job, rather than settling for a lower-level one (wiki inference). That pattern fits a squeeze on entry-level hiring rather than a fall in the value of the skills. It sits alongside the Census finding that graduates of the most AI-exposed majors increasingly start in retail and food service ([[graduating-into-disruption-census]]); the NY Fed table is a one-year cross-section of levels, while the Census paper measures changes after ChatGPT, so the two are not directly comparable.

### Smaller discrepancies with other wiki pages

- [[columbia-cs-ai-disruption]] cites NY Fed data (2024) ranking computer science 5th of 74 majors at 7%. This file ranks it 4th of 73 at 7.0%, probably a later data year; computer engineering is 2nd at 7.8% in both.
- [[safest-college-degrees-forbes]] compares Q2 2026's 5.6% with a 4.2% "overall" rate. The file's all-workers series for June 2026 is 4.07% (source: College-labor-data.xlsx), so Forbes's 4.2% likely comes from a different headline series. The comparison mixes ages either way, as that page already cautions.

## Implications for CTE

These are the wiki's inferences, not claims made by the source.

1. **Watch underemployment, not just unemployment.** For students weighing a four-year degree, more than two in five recent graduates are in jobs that don't need one. That is the relevant comparison for CTE pathways that lead directly to skilled work.
2. **CTE completers are invisible in this benchmark.** Public debate about "how new graduates are doing" draws on bachelor's-only data. CTE programs need their own outcome data to be part of that conversation ([[education-to-workforce-data-systems]]).
3. **Bachelor's-level CTE-adjacent fields do well.** Nursing, construction services, accounting, and civil engineering combine low unemployment with low underemployment. Engineering technologies has very low unemployment but high underemployment (44%), suggesting many of its graduates work in jobs that don't formally require the degree.
4. **Don't promise the degree pays more than it used to.** The premium over a high-school diploma is real (about 1.5×) but the median graduate's real pay has been flat for 35 years.
5. **Quarterly updates make this a useful tracking source.** The next releases (February, May 2027) will show whether the Q3 improvement holds.
