# AI Is Reshaping the Economy — Good Luck Measuring How (Casselman, NYT)

**Summary**: A July 2026 New York Times piece by Ben Casselman surveying why economists cannot yet agree on AI's real effect on jobs, wages, or productivity — government statistics are outdated and underfunded, private data sources conflict with each other, and non-AI shocks (COVID, interest rates, immigration policy) make attribution nearly impossible.

**Sources**: `raw/misc/A.I. Is Reshaping the Economy. Good Luck Measuring How.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-07-02

## Overview

Casselman's core claim is not that AI's economic effects are small — it's that they are currently unmeasurable with confidence, and by the time measurement catches up, it may be too late for policymakers to respond. "The stakes are super high," Economic Innovation Group's Nathan Goldschlag tells the Times. "Getting the policy right is going to depend on getting the measurement right." (source: A.I. Is Reshaping the Economy. Good Luck Measuring How.md)

This is a meta-level contribution to the wiki: rather than adding another data point about AI's labor market effect, it explains *why* the data points the wiki has already collected (e.g. [[anthropic-labor-market-index]], [[bcg-ai-reshapes-jobs]], [[stanford-del-ai-economic-indicators]]) diverge so sharply from one another.

## Why the Signals Conflict

**Government data is structurally outdated.** The Bureau of Labor Statistics' monthly jobs report breaks out manufacturing, retail, and construction — but not technology, which is scattered across categories like "information" alongside newspapers and film studios. The most recent detailed occupational breakdown is from May 2025 — over a year old at time of publication, "an eternity ago in the fast-evolving world of A.I." (source: A.I. Is Reshaping the Economy. Good Luck Measuring How.md)

**Exposure measures disagree even in direction.** A Northwestern/American University study (cited in the article) found that switching between different AI-exposure measures could flip not just the *magnitude* but the *sign* of AI's estimated employment effect — hurting employment under some measures, helping under others. Economist Michelle Yin: "It's like going to the doctor and getting three different diagnoses for the same condition." (source: A.I. Is Reshaping the Economy. Good Luck Measuring How.md)

**Private data sources conflict too.** The Stanford Digital Economy Lab, using ADP payroll data, found entry-level jobs declining sharply in the most AI-exposed sectors since 2022 — Erik Brynjolfsson calls it a "canary in the coal mine." But research from Ramp (expense-management data) and Revelio Labs found that companies using AI *most intensely* were adding jobs *faster* than slower adopters. Both data sets are non-representative in different directions: ADP skews toward large, established firms; Ramp skews toward tech-savvy firms.

**Federal statistical capacity is shrinking.** Falling survey response rates and budget shortfalls are degrading the government's ability to measure what's happening in real time. Former BLS commissioner Erika McEntarfer: "The data we're currently using to understand A.I.'s impact on the labor market is in jeopardy because of funding shortfalls." A bipartisan Senate bill (Kelly, Banks, Young) introduced in June 2026 would expand federal AI labor-market data collection and require an annual impact report.

## The J-Curve Explanation

Brynjolfsson's J-curve theory — companies initially become *less* productive while experimenting with a new general-purpose technology, then see rapid gains once they learn to use it — is offered as one explanation for why the current signals are so mixed: most companies may still be on the downward part of the J. This directly echoes [[ai-economics-scan-2026]]'s "still early days" framing and Bryan Alexander's three-scenario ambiguity (jobs created / jobs destroyed / jobs unchanged in count).

## What Isn't in Dispute

Researchers broadly agree on two things: (1) AI's effect on the *aggregate* economy has been limited so far, and (2) untangling AI's effect from other simultaneous shocks — COVID, high interest rates, immigration and trade policy swings — will only get *easier* over time as those other shocks fade, even as the AI signal itself keeps changing.

## Relationship to the Wiki's Existing Evidence

This piece functions as an epistemic caveat layered over the wiki's growing labor-market evidence base rather than a new empirical claim. It should be read alongside, not instead of:

- [[anthropic-labor-market-index]] and [[stanford-del-ai-economic-indicators]] — both are exactly the kind of "private data source" the article warns can't be assumed representative
- [[bcg-ai-reshapes-jobs]] and [[ai-occupational-exposure-index]] — both use AI-exposure measures of the type the article says can flip sign depending on methodology
- [[ai-economics-scan-2026]] — Bryan Alexander's scan reaches the same "still early days, signals contradictory" conclusion via a different route (news/signal aggregation rather than a measurement-methodology critique)

## Implications for CTE

1. **Occupational exposure indices (AIOE, Task Routinization) used throughout the wiki's CTE sources carry real methodological uncertainty.** The [[ai-occupational-exposure-index]] cluster-grouping rankings should be read as directional evidence, not precise forecasts — the underlying measurement technology is contested even among economists.
2. **CTE data literacy should include measurement skepticism.** If professional economists cannot agree on which direction AI is pushing employment, CTE programs teaching data analysis or business intelligence should model that same epistemic humility rather than presenting single-source AI-impact statistics as settled fact.
3. **Policy timing risk is real for CTE funding decisions.** Goldschlag's warning that "by the time the data is clear, it could be too late for policymakers to figure out how to respond" applies directly to CTE program funding and curriculum decisions — waiting for definitive data before adapting programs carries its own risk.
