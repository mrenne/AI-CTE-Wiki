# What Will Be Scarce?

**Summary**: A 2026 Substack essay by University of Chicago economist Alex Imas arguing that AI will trigger a structural shift toward a "relational sector" — where human involvement is the product itself — drawing on structural change economics, mimetic desire theory, and experimental data showing AI undermines the exclusivity premium of goods.

**Sources**: `raw/blog/What will be scarce_.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-05-04

---

## Overview

*What Will Be Scarce?* was published April 13, 2026 on Alex Imas's Substack. Imas is an economist at the University of Chicago Booth School of Business whose research focuses on behavioral economics, decision-making, and consumer preferences. The essay builds on structural change economics and Imas's own experimental research on mimetic desire to argue against the "AI eliminates labor" thesis.

The essay is cited and synthesized by Ezra Klein in the NYT piece [[ai-job-apocalypse-klein]] and pairs with Bryan Alexander's horizon scan [[ai-economics-scan-2026]] as a more theoretically grounded economic framing.

## Core Argument

The standard AI labor narrative treats the economy as a fixed set of jobs: AI can do X, so workers who do X are replaced. Imas rejects this as a static view. The historical pattern of structural change — agriculture → manufacturing → services — shows that when technology makes one type of production cheap, the economy doesn't collapse; it transforms toward what rising incomes make people want more of.

> "The answer to any question about the future economics of advanced AI begins with identifying what becomes scarce." (source: What will be scarce_.md)

The claim: AI makes cognitive commodities abundant (briefs, images, code, diagnoses). Abundance in one sector historically triggers demand shifts toward sectors with higher income elasticity. The sector with the highest income elasticity in a post-commodity economy is the **relational sector** — where the human element is inseparable from the value.

## The Structural Change Framework

Imas draws on Comin, Lashkari & Mestieri (*Econometrica*, 2021), who model the historical transition from agriculture to manufacturing to services. Their key finding: **income effects account for over 75% of observed patterns of structural change**. Price effects explain only about a quarter. Rising real income is the main driver; people shift toward fundamentally different goods as they get richer.

Applied to AI: falling prices in automated sectors raise real income, and rising income shifts demand toward sectors with higher income elasticity. The relational sector — care, hospitality, artisanal production, education, therapy — has high income elasticity because demand for human-provided goods is never fully satiated when incomes rise.

Evidence from the BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey (2022): households in the top income quintile spend 4.3× more in total than those in the bottom quintile. In categories with a strong relational component (in-person dining, entertainment, education), the ratio is substantially higher. Rich households don't just buy more stuff — they shift toward goods where the human element matters more. (source: What will be scarce_.md)

## Mimetic Desire as the Behavioral Mechanism

Why does the relational sector have especially high income elasticity? Imas grounds this in **mimetic desire** (René Girard): desire is not just a function of what we want intrinsically, but of what others want and cannot have. This makes relational goods inherently comparative and hard to satiate — the opposite of commodity goods that satisfy basic needs.

Imas and Madarasz provide experimental support (source: What will be scarce_.md):
- In a controlled experiment, willingness to pay **roughly doubled** when subjects learned that random others would be excluded from a product — even when the product was identical and exclusion was random
- The demand curve shifted substantially as the prospect of exclusion increased
- This effect is driven by a "pure preference for having something others don't" — distinct from status signaling or scarcity heuristics

New work with Graelin Mandel tests AI's specific effect on this premium (source: What will be scarce_.md):
- Subjects bid for physical art prints described as human-made or AI-generated
- Human-made artwork: exclusivity (one copy vs. many) increased value by **44%**
- AI-generated artwork: the same exclusivity manipulation increased value by only **21%**
- Conclusion: AI involvement makes goods feel inherently reproducible, undermining the exclusivity premium regardless of actual scarcity

The implication: as AI produces more commodity-quality content, the premium on provably human-made goods and services rises. The relational sector gets more valuable, not less, as AI capability improves.

## The Starbucks Case Study

The essay opens with a concrete illustration. Starbucks spent years automating coffee production to cut costs, reducing barista headcount and standardizing processes. CEO Brian Niccol reversed course: hiring more baristas per store, reintroducing handwritten notes on cups, bringing back ceramic cups and comfortable seating. The conclusion was that "small details and hospitality drive satisfaction." (source: What will be scarce_.md)

The hospitality was not separable from the coffee. Automation improved the commodity; it degraded the product.

## Baumol's Cost Disease as a Feature

The relational sector resists productivity improvement because the human element *is* the service. A therapist cannot serve twice as many clients per hour. This creates **Baumol's cost disease**: the stagnant sector gets relatively expensive.

In standard economic analysis, this is a problem. Imas argues that in the context of AI, it is a **feature**:

- Commodity sectors get cheaper → real income rises
- Real income rise → spending shifts toward high-income-elasticity sectors
- The relational sector is exactly that sector
- Its relative expensiveness sustains wages for relational workers
- The "stagnant" sector absorbs a growing share of expenditure and employment precisely because it cannot be automated

"The relative expense of human services stops being a budget problem and starts being a labor market solution." (source: What will be scarce_.md)

## What the Relational Sector Looks Like

Jobs already in the relational sector (and growing): nurses, therapists, teachers, boutique fitness instructors, personal chefs, bespoke tailors, craft brewers, live performers, spiritual guides, childcare workers, hospitality and care work broadly.

Emerging roles: experience designers, human-AI collaboration artists, provenance certifiers, community curators. And many haven't been invented yet — as was true of most jobs in 1940.

The common objection — "not everyone can be an artist" — misunderstands the claim. The relational sector doesn't require Picasso. It requires workers "whose involvement makes the product feel like it was made for someone, by someone." (source: What will be scarce_.md)

## Caveats

Imas is explicit about the limits of the thesis (source: What will be scarce_.md):

1. **This applies most to rich countries.** Developing economies built on commodity production for wealthy nations face a more complicated picture — potentially devastating, as AI eliminates the export demand that historically funded their development.

2. **Labor's aggregate share may still fall.** The relational sector can grow as a share of employment even while labor's total share of national income declines. The claim is about sectoral reallocation, not the labor/capital income split.

3. **The transition requires income to fund it.** The mechanism depends on rising real incomes shifting demand. If AI-driven gains are captured primarily by capital owners rather than distributed broadly, the demand shift may not materialize — leading to the demand collapse scenario Imas discusses in a companion essay.
