# AI Unplugged: The Hype and Hope in Education Futures

**Summary**: A June 2026 MindShare TV podcast interview with Dr. Stephen Murgatroyd, author of *AI Unplugged* (Routledge), offering a Canadian practitioner-academic perspective on AI in education — including a "build, analyze, defend" assessment scaffold, a strong historical skepticism argument, Canadian equity and access data, Western cultural bias in AI, and an indigenous school AI case study.

## Overview

Published June 4, 2026 on MindShareTV (MindShare Learning, Canada). Host: Robert Merloti. Guest: Dr. Stephen Murgatroyd — education futurist, University of Alberta, former dean at Athabasca University. *AI Unplugged: The Hype and Hope in Education Futures* (Routledge, co-authored with John-Claude Couture) is his 50th book and draws on seven years of teaching AI to school leaders and aspiring school leaders. This is the first Canadian, non-US source in the wiki. (source: New Book Podcast: AI Unplugged.md)

## Historical Skepticism: "We've Had This Conversation Since 1927"

Murgatroyd explicitly challenges AI exceptionalism:

> "I've been there, done that. We've had this conversation about edtech since 1927 — skimmers, teaching machines right through. So if you look at the history, every time something has happened… I did my master's research on interactive whiteboards. 'Oh, this is going to revolutionize teaching.' What's really going to make the difference is when teachers and students collaborate to create their own versions of what should be happening in schools."

He frames each edtech wave — from teaching machines to smartboards to AI — as a failed revolution that placed technology at the center and pushed students and teachers to the margins. His view: the revolution, if any, is about empowerment, not technology. "The revolution is not about technology at all and never has been and never will be. It's about empowerment of students and teachers working together."

This is an important counterpoint to the wiki's generally AI-disruption-affirming sources. Murgatroyd would not deny that AI has new capabilities — he is explicitly preparing school leaders for it — but he questions whether capability changes produce educational transformation without corresponding changes in institutional structures and teacher agency. His historical lens suggests extrapolating from past edtech waves to be appropriately cautious about transformation claims. (source: New Book Podcast: AI Unplugged.md)

## The Interregnum Frame

Murgatroyd offers a distinctive temporal framing for the current moment:

> "I think we're in an interregnum between the current kind of neoliberal top-down control system and an emerging system where teachers have to fight back and where kids are fighting back already… We're caught in this highly distressful kind of gray messy interregnant space."

The word "interregnum" — the period between two ruling orders when normal governance is suspended — captures something the wiki's more forward-looking frameworks miss: that the transition is disorienting, that neither the old system nor the new one is yet fully operational, and that the distress is a structural feature rather than a temporary adjustment cost.

Students confirm this directly: "We're not learning in the way that we want to learn. We're not engaged in this learning. Grades are more important than learning." Murgatroyd is building an international network of kids and teachers working together to "navigate a journey out of this interregnant space." (source: New Book Podcast: AI Unplugged.md)

## Assessment Redesign: The "Build → Analyze → Defend" Scaffold

The most specific and actionable pedagogical contribution of this source is a three-stage assessment scaffold:

1. **Build**: Students produce something — an essay, a project, an analysis — with AI assistance
2. **Analyze**: Students critically interrogate what they built — what did AI contribute? What's accurate? What's missing?
3. **Defend**: Students orally defend the work — presenting it to peers, explaining choices, accounting for gaps

> "Build, analyze, defend is the scaffolding process that actually increases cognition. If you don't do that, then we're likely to see a reduction in cognitive capabilities."

This maps directly onto [[agentivism]]'s four mechanisms: delegated agency (build), epistemic monitoring (analyze), reconstructive internalization (defend), and transfer under reduced support (the oral defense, which requires independent capability). It is a classroom-ready implementation of Agentivism — simpler and more explicitly sequenced than Nurenberg's [[middle-path-ai-literacy-nurenberg]] AI audit, but structurally aligned.

Murgatroyd frames the underlying problem as an assessment failure, not an AI adoption failure: "AI has exposed how awful our assessment practices are — how they're about assessing retention of knowledge rather than applied knowledge or knowledge as understanding." Portfolio assessment — oral work, video, poetry, images, peer presentation — is his systemic response. He frames learning itself as "a 50-year apprenticeship," not a credential-completion event. (source: New Book Podcast: AI Unplugged.md)

## AI Detection Software: Ineffective and Legally Risky

Murgatroyd makes a sharp practical claim directed at school administrators:

> "AI detection software doesn't work. You're actually opening yourself to legal jeopardy. There are lots of cases where students are suing the school system or the university or college for falsely accusing them of plagiarism and academic misconduct."

This goes beyond the widely-cited unreliability of AI detection tools (false positive rates have been documented in multiple studies). Murgatroyd is flagging a legal liability dimension that most school policy discussions have not yet integrated: institutions that rely on AI detection as an enforcement mechanism are potentially creating discrimination claims when false positives damage students' academic records.

His alternative: design assessment so that it cannot be gamed by AI rather than trying to detect and penalize AI use after the fact. The "build, analyze, defend" scaffold is exactly this — oral defense and real-time interrogation make the AI-generation question largely irrelevant. (source: New Book Podcast: AI Unplugged.md)

## Students as Knowledgeable Participants

Murgatroyd's work with the Toronto District School Board's Black Leadership Development Program produced a striking finding about student AI knowledge:

> "Their biggest issue is nobody's listening to us. We actually know more about AI than most of the adults in this school system. We know what some of the concerns are about cognitive offloading, about cheating, about abuse, about bias, about hallucination."

Students explicitly demanded: "Do not develop the Toronto school board policy on AI without involving us in the development of that policy."

This is a student agency claim that connects to [[agentivism]]'s core argument about learner epistemic responsibility — and inverts the usual framing in which adults design AI literacy programs for students who lack the knowledge to participate. The Toronto students are ahead of their institutions. It also complicates the [[illusion-of-understanding]] finding: that study documented 14-year-olds in a structured science task showing chance-level AI discrimination; this finding suggests older students actively working with AI — and in a racial equity leadership context — develop a more sophisticated critical stance. (source: New Book Podcast: AI Unplugged.md)

## Teacher Working Conditions as the Real Adoption Constraint

Murgatroyd grounds the 40–50% of teachers not engaging with AI in structural conditions, not personal resistance:

> "Alberta had the longest and largest teacher strike in its history that ended up with the government using the notwithstanding clause to force teachers back to work with no settlement… Huge classrooms, highly complex classrooms, very few supports. I don't blame teachers for saying 'Do not give me one more thing to do.'"

This provides institutional grounding for the educator AI adoption data in [[stanford-ai-index-education-2026]]. The policy vacuum (no teacher training standards, 6% of teachers with clear AI policies) is not just an oversight — it is occurring in a context of severe workforce stress. Asking teachers to integrate AI without structural support, time, and compensation is not just ineffective; it is asking the most strained professionals in the system to absorb additional cognitive and planning load.

His "lighthouses" — pockets of innovation that are succeeding — share a common feature: they focus AI not on generic use but on supporting specific groups of students with identifiable needs (newcomers, indigenous students, students with special needs, students struggling with specific subjects). (source: New Book Podcast: AI Unplugged.md)

## Canadian Equity and Access Data

Murgatroyd provides the most specific access equity data in the wiki for a G7 country:

- **650,000 Canadian households** (~4% of the population) have no internet access — concentrated in northern Ontario, northern Quebec, northern Manitoba, northern BC, and Alberta's north
- Who lives there: primarily indigenous communities and rural low-income populations
- **Canada ranks among the 5 most expensive jurisdictions globally** for mobile phone and broadband costs
- Many powerful AI tools require payment — "even $20/month is a huge amount of money if you don't have it"
- His own annual AI/internet/technology software bill: ~$5,000 — "I teach this stuff, so I need access to it. But most teachers don't have that kind of money, nor should they be asked to pay for it."
- As AI companies move toward profitability, costs will rise: "Some of them haven't made any money so far."

This enriches the [[ai-privacy-and-institutional-adoption]] infrastructure gap section with specific international data. The Canadian figures likely understate the problem in less affluent countries. (source: New Book Podcast: AI Unplugged.md)

## Western Cultural Bias and Indigenous Data Sovereignty

Murgatroyd identifies a structural bias in AI outputs that goes beyond the familiar accuracy and hallucination concerns:

> "A lot of the AI slop is based on a kind of western imperialist colonialist cultural view. If you talk to ChatGPT or Gemini about how the treaties got written and made and signed and agreed to in Canada, it's basically nonsense."

His proposed response: data sovereignty — localized AI systems built on locally controlled data rather than cloud-based systems trained on predominantly Western, English-language corpora. He is advising a British Columbia indigenous LMS provider whose systems use local, sovereign, personal data to address both the privacy issue and the cultural accuracy problem.

**Treaty 8 school case study**: A northern Alberta indigenous school in Treaty 8 territory cannot find teachers or educational assistants, so AI is being used as a necessity, not an enhancement. They are co-designing learning supports in Cree language, based on indigenous storytelling, with indigenous cultural resources attached — developed collaboratively with students and teachers. This is an edge case that reframes the AI equity conversation: rather than AI as a threat to indigenous learning or a luxury for well-resourced schools, it becomes the only available infrastructure for communities that colonial resource allocation has left without human educational capacity. (source: New Book Podcast: AI Unplugged.md)

## Entry-Level Jobs and the Ecosystem Reimagination

Murgatroyd confirms the entry-level hiring pipeline problem from a practitioner perspective:

> "Kids leaving schools can't get the entry-level jobs whether they've left college, university, or high school — can't get access to the entry-level jobs they used to be able to get access to because AI is taking a lot of that work."

He frames the response as an ecosystem redesign — the school is not a standalone institution but part of a "learning knowledge certification skills development system." One data point he offers: Deloitte and Shopify are now hiring directly from high school, preferring to train young workers rather than wait for university or college credentialing. This is an adaptive employer response to the entry-level pipeline shift — consistent with [[sinead-bovell-job-market]]'s independence era argument but pointing to large employers making the move, not just independent workers.

His proposed new role for school leaders: from "leader of learning and manager of resources" to "anticipation, collaboration, and eco-network building for learning, assessment, and skill development." The school principal as an ecosystem connector rather than an institutional manager. (source: New Book Podcast: AI Unplugged.md)
