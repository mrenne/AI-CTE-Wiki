## Overview

This is an update to the Alabama Data Scholars program already documented in [[senate-hearing-ai-k12-education]] via QuantHub CEO Joshua Jones's June 2026 Senate testimony. Where the hearing summary described the program's structure and scale in general terms (100+ paid internships, semester-cadence curriculum refresh), this source provides the underlying curriculum-design mechanics and the specific 2026 cohort numbers. (source: Preparing Students for AI & Data Careers.md)

## The 2026 Cohort

52 student interns placed with 23 Alabama organizations spanning technology companies, healthcare organizations, manufacturers, nonprofits, financial institutions, and government agencies.

## Curriculum Built From Employer Demand, Not Assumptions

QuantHub analyzed internship postings across dozens of participating organizations to identify which technical and professional skills appeared most frequently, then built the curriculum directly from that analysis — rather than starting from a generic data-science syllabus and hoping it matched employer need. The result: a curriculum mapped to 43 internship roles across 20 industries, organized into 17 major skill categories.

**Top employer-demanded skill categories** (number of employers requesting): Data Analytics & Business Intelligence (25), Data Visualization & Dashboards (18), Automation & AI Workflows (17), Generative AI & AI Tools (16), Storytelling & Stakeholder Communication (14), SQL & Database Skills (12), Excel (9), AI Agents & Chatbots (8), Python (5), Machine Learning & Predictive AI (4).

**Share of employer internship projects requesting each skill area**: Data Analytics & Business Intelligence 74%, AI & Emerging Technologies 65%, Data Visualization & Storytelling 42%, Python & SQL 35%, Database Management 26%, Generative AI Tools 26%, Dashboards 23%, Excel & Data Wrangling 19%, Machine Learning 16%, Task Automation 16%.

## Shared Foundation, Then Specialization

Every Data Scholar completes **Advanced AI Foundations** in the first two weeks — covering business challenges and AI applications, the history and current state of AI, fundamental AI system characteristics, generative AI use cases in professional workflows, prompt engineering, and ethical/security considerations. This establishes common AI literacy before students diverge into occupation-specific tracks. (source: Preparing Students for AI & Data Careers.md)

Students then choose between:

- **Track A: AI & Technology** — algorithmic thinking, Python, SQL, data wrangling, AI output evaluation, critical thinking for AI, prompt engineering, reasoning strategies, context engineering, human-AI collaboration, descriptive statistics, Excel analysis, visualization. Prepares students for software development, technical AI applications, automation, and emerging-technology internships.
- **Track B: Data & Business Analytics** — Excel workspaces, descriptive statistics, advanced formulas, pivot tables, nested logic, AI-assisted data analysis, project planning, prompt engineering, context engineering, AI tool evaluation, business intelligence. Prepares students for analytics, operations, finance, marketing, consulting, and organizational decision-support internships.

This two-track structure operationalizes the "half supply chain, half AI" idea Dr. Kourosh Bahrami described in [[cems-augmented-leadership]] — occupation-specific AI fluency built on top of a shared literacy foundation, rather than either a generic "AI class" or a fully bespoke curriculum per role.

## Program Leadership and Rural Access Mission

Program Director Kelsey Bickett cultivates the employer partnerships; mentor Lucas Hershberger connects coursework to internship experience. CEO Joshua Jones has explicitly championed expanding AI/data literacy access into rural Alabama communities, framing geography as a barrier the program is designed to remove — directly relevant to the wiki's recurring digital-divide/rural-access thread (see [[ai-privacy-and-institutional-adoption]]).

## Implications for CTE

1. **Employer-demand-mapped curriculum design is replicable and auditable.** The 43-role/17-category framework is a concrete template other CTE programs could adapt — start from actual internship postings in a target region, not a generic AI curriculum.
2. **A shared-foundation-then-track structure balances AI literacy breadth with occupational specificity** — directly addressing the tension [[applied-co-intelligence-model]] identifies between standalone AI courses (too generic) and fully occupation-embedded AI content (too narrow to scale).
3. **Rural access remains an explicit, named program goal**, not an afterthought — reinforcing that CTE AI-workforce pipelines require deliberate geographic equity design, consistent with [[ai-privacy-and-institutional-adoption]]'s broader access-barrier findings.
