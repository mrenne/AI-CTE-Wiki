# CESifo: Recent-Grad Unemployment Shows No AI Effect (Yet)

**Summary**: An Ars Technica report (September 2026) on a CESifo working paper (Fairlie & Wu) finding that summer 2026 unemployment among recent US college graduates, 7.3%, was within the normal 2022–2024 range and not significantly different from non-college peers, older graduates, or less AI-exposed groups. It is the wiki's clearest counter-signal to the entry-level disruption thesis, though it measures unemployment, a different outcome from the studies it appears to contradict.

## About this source

Ars Technica article by Kyle Orland (September 25, 2026) summarizing the CESifo working paper *The Early Impacts of AI on Employment Among Recent College Graduates* by Robert Fairlie and Jane Wu (source: AI was supposed to hit new grads hard. So far, unemployment data says otherwise..md). **This is a secondhand summary**: the wiki has not ingested the paper itself, so details of methods and robustness come through the journalist's account.

---

## Why 2026 was the test year

The authors chose recent graduates because "changes in labor demand may first appear through reductions in hiring." They point to reasons 2026's graduates should be more at risk than earlier cohorts (source: AI was supposed to hit new grads hard. So far, unemployment data says otherwise..md):
- a sharp recent rise in firms reporting "replacing a large number of employee tasks with AI" in a Census survey;
- broad increases in AI spending per employee and ChatGPT Enterprise token use over the past year;
- prominent predictions. Marc Andreessen said AI "until December [2025] was not actually good enough to do any of the jobs that they're actually cutting," and BlackRock CEO Larry Fink warned this year's graduates could see "the highest unemployment rate among them in years—even without a recession."

---

## Findings

Using Current Population Survey microdata on bachelor's-degree holders aged 22–25 who are not pursuing further degrees (source: AI was supposed to hit new grads hard. So far, unemployment data says otherwise..md):
- **Summer 2026 unemployment for recent graduates was 7.3%**, within the range of earlier summers: from 6.3% in 2022 to 7.8% in 2024.
- The result holds when discouraged workers, who "want a job" but aren't looking, are included.
- Compared with same-age non-college workers, older graduates (30–49), and by AI exposure, trend differences over 2022–2026 were almost all **not statistically significant**.
- The authors' verdict: "there is no evidence of any significant, widespread displacement or reduction in hiring of recent college graduates in absolute or relative levels."

**Their own caution:** "if the intensity of AI use in the workplace continues to increase, the graduating classes of 2027 and later might be more affected than the class of 2026" (source: AI was supposed to hit new grads hard. So far, unemployment data says otherwise..md).

## ⚠ Contradiction with the wiki's entry-level evidence

On its face this conflicts with [[canaries-coal-mine-brynjolfsson]] (19% employment shortfall for young workers in exposed occupations), [[graduating-into-disruption-census]] (−5 points initial employment for the most-exposed majors), and [[ai-labor-demand-41-countries]] (junior share −1.9 points at adopting firms).

**Why they can all be true.** The studies measure different outcomes:
- **Unemployment vs. employment in specific jobs.** The Census paper found graduates of exposed majors "remain strongly attached to the labor market" but start in lower-paying sectors, and that "modest employment or unemployment effects can coexist with substantial deterioration" in job quality (source: Gradiating into Disruption.pdf). A graduate who ends up in retail instead of software counts as *employed* in the CPS.
- **Occupation-level counts vs. economy-wide rates.** Canaries tracks employment *within* AI-exposed occupations in ADP payroll data. Ars Technica's explanation: ADP "looks at the total supply of jobs in various fields," while the unemployment rate reflects overall demand, which "could easily shift even if the supply of jobs in certain fields starts contracting" (source: AI was supposed to hit new grads hard. So far, unemployment data says otherwise..md).
- **Firm composition vs. total headcount.** [[ai-labor-demand-41-countries]] finds adopters shift toward seniors *without* cutting total employment (source: HowDoesAIChangeLaborDemand.pdf).
- **Concentration.** The Census effects sit in the top tenth of exposed majors. A broad recent-graduate unemployment rate can dilute a concentrated effect. This is an inference; the Ars summary doesn't say how finely CESifo split by exposure.

**What remains a genuine disagreement.** CESifo reports that recent graduates in more AI-exposed groups did *not* fare significantly worse on unemployment. That is a direct test, and it did not find the pattern. This page records the conflict rather than resolving it.

**Two figures to reconcile.** The NY Fed's 5.6% recent-graduate unemployment ([[safest-college-degrees-forbes]], Q2 2026) and CESifo's 7.3% (summer 2026) differ because of season and definition: summer rates spike as new graduates enter, and the age ranges and data sources differ. They are not in conflict.

## Implications for CTE

1. **Avoid overclaiming.** CTE advocacy that says "AI is destroying entry-level jobs for college graduates" now has a credible counter-study. The defensible claim is narrower: AI-exposed *fields* show weaker entry and worse first jobs, while overall graduate unemployment has not (yet) moved.
2. **Job quality, not joblessness, is the outcome to watch.** For career advising, the relevant risk for exposed fields is underemployment and slower starts ([[no-country-young-grads-burning-glass]], [[graduating-into-disruption-census]]), not headline unemployment.
3. **The class of 2027 is the next test.** The authors expect effects may appear later if workplace AI intensity keeps rising. The wiki should re-check this when 2027 data arrive.

## A Possible Explanation: Displacement Waits for Recessions (October 2026)

[[goldman-ai-displacement-cyclical]] offers a reason recent-graduate unemployment could stay normal even if AI is reducing labor demand. Historically, "automation-related labor disruption is limited when the overall economy is healthy but accelerates during cyclical slowdowns," and Goldman attributes the limited impact so far partly to the economy's resilience in 2026 (source: AI could displace one in 11 workers, Goldman says, but the layoffs ar….md). If so, the CESifo authors' caution that later graduating classes "might be more affected" could depend as much on the business cycle as on rising AI use.
