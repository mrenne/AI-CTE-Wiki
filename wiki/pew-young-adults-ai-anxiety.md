# Pew: Young Adults Are Increasingly Wary of AI, Concerned It Will Take Jobs

**Summary**: A Pew Research Center survey (August 2026, fielded June 22-28) finding that 52% of US adults are now more concerned than excited about AI (up from 37% in 2021), that 71% believe AI will lead to fewer jobs (up from 64% in 2024), and — most significantly for this wiki — that adults under 30 are the only age group whose AI concern is still climbing while other age groups' concern has plateaued since roughly 2023. The wiki's first direct attitudinal/sentiment data on AI and jobs, complementing its existing behavioral evidence.

**Sources**: `raw/misc/Young adults in the U.S. are increasingly wary of AI, concerned it will take jobs.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-08-23

## About this source

A Pew Research Center short-read (Colleen McClain, August 18, 2026), based on a nationally representative survey of 3,488 US adults from Pew's American Trends Panel, fielded June 22-28, 2026. Pew has asked a version of this question since 2021, giving the wiki its first multi-year attitudinal trend line on AI sentiment — distinct from and complementary to the behavioral/revealed-preference evidence already documented in [[nyt-teens-trade-classes]] and the Gallup/Lumina major-switching data cited in [[broken-ladder]].

## Key findings

**Overall AI sentiment has shifted sharply toward concern.** 52% of US adults say increased AI use in daily life makes them more concerned than excited — up from 37% in 2021. Only 9% are more excited than concerned (down from 18% in 2021); 37% are equally concerned and excited. The shift happened mostly between 2022 and 2023 (jumping from 38% to 52% concerned) and has been roughly stable since.

**Young adults are the one age group where concern is still rising, not plateaued.** This is the finding most relevant to the wiki. For adults 30 and older, most of the increase in concern happened in 2021-2023 and has since leveled off. For **adults under 30**, concern has kept climbing every year: 31% (2021) → 34% (2022) → 42% (2023) → 39% (2024) → 47% (2025) → **55% (2026)**. By 2026, adults under 30 are now *more* concerned than every other age group except those 65+, having started as the *least* concerned age group in 2021. Excitement about AI among adults under 30 has fallen in parallel: 25% (2021) down to 11% (2026).

**Belief that AI will reduce the number of jobs has grown sharply, and young adults have converged with older adults on this view.** 71% of all US adults now think AI will lead to fewer jobs over the next two decades, up from 64% in 2024. Only 5% think it will create more jobs. Among adults under 30 specifically, this view rose from 61% (2024) to **73% (2026)** — meaning young adults, who were previously somewhat less pessimistic than older cohorts on this specific question, are now statistically indistinguishable from the 30-64 age bracket and slightly *more* pessimistic than adults 65+.

**Young adults' AI skepticism extends beyond jobs to a broader "bad for me" framing.** Pew's related work (cited in this piece) finds adults under 30 are the age group most likely to think AI will be bad for society and for them personally, most likely to think AI will make connection and creativity harder to come by, and — despite using chatbots at rates comparable to or higher than older adults — just as likely to say chatbots *hurt* their own creativity as to say the tools help it.

## Why this matters for the wiki

This is the wiki's **first direct sentiment-survey evidence** on AI and jobs, as distinct from behavioral evidence (trade enrollment, major-switching) or labor-market outcome data (employment, hiring rates). It closes a real gap: the wiki has extensively documented what young people are *doing* in response to AI-driven career anxiety ([[nyt-teens-trade-classes]], the Gallup/Lumina 47%-considered-switching-majors finding in [[broken-ladder]] and [[spelhaug-microsoft-elevate]]) but had no systematic, multi-year *attitudinal* trend line confirming that the underlying worry is real, rising, and age-concentrated rather than a media narrative built on anecdote.

The convergence is notable: **stated worry (Pew, this source) and revealed behavior (NYT trade-pivot video, Gallup/Lumina major-switching survey) point in the same direction, from different methodologies, in the same population.** This is exactly the kind of independent-methodology corroboration the wiki's citation practice treats as strong evidence (see similar patterns noted in [[senate-hearing-ai-k12-education]] and [[cems-augmented-leadership]]).

## An open question this source raises but doesn't answer

Pew's data shows *belief* that AI will reduce jobs (71%, general/future-oriented) is now nearly universal and only loosely age-differentiated at that level — the sharper age gradient is in *personal concern/excitement* framing, not the abstract "will AI reduce jobs" question. This suggests young adults' distinctive anxiety is less about disagreeing with older adults on the macro question and more about a personal stake in the outcome — consistent with the "no path is safe" psychological frame documented in [[nyt-teens-trade-classes]], but the Pew data cannot itself distinguish whether this reflects accurate risk perception, media/social amplification, or something else. The wiki should treat this as attitudinal corroboration of *that a real anxiety exists and is concentrated among young adults*, not as new evidence about whether that anxiety is empirically well-calibrated.

## Implications for CTE

1. **CTE recruitment and counseling messaging is landing on an audience whose baseline anxiety about AI and jobs is now higher, and still rising, more than any other age group.** This strengthens the case (already made via [[nyt-teens-trade-classes]]) that CTE's "AI hedge" framing resonates with where this cohort already is emotionally, rather than needing to manufacture concern.
2. **The gap between stated belief (71% think AI reduces jobs) and actual behavioral response (a minority of students, per [[nyt-teens-trade-classes]] and [[broken-ladder]]'s Gallup/Lumina data, have acted on it) is itself a CTE opportunity.** Widespread belief without proportional behavioral response suggests many students who are anxious about AI have not yet been exposed to CTE as a concrete alternative — reinforcing the "CTE as discovery mechanism" thesis in [[nyt-teens-trade-classes]] and [[cte-futures-aci-webinar]].
3. **The finding that young adults see AI as actively harming their own creativity (not just their jobs) is a data point relevant to [[cognitive-debt]] and [[agentivism]]** — self-reported perception of creative/cognitive harm from AI use, from the population most exposed to it, corroborating (though not proving) the cognitive-debt mechanism from the attitudinal side rather than the neurological (EEG) side documented in [[your-brain-on-chatgpt]].
