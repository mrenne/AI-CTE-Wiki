# The Middle Path of AI Literacy (Nurenberg, 2026)

**Summary**: A first-person practitioner account by a high school English teacher who used AI itself as the curriculum object — having students discover AI's failures through direct comparison — and replaced AI bans with AI audits requiring students to demonstrate critical evaluation of every tool they used.

**Sources**: `raw/blog/Why the Middle Path of AI Literacy May Be the Future of English Class.md`, `raw/misc/Senate Hearing on Artificial Intelligence and K-12 Education.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-07-08

---

## Overview

*Why the 'Middle Path' of AI Literacy May Be the Future of English Class* was published May 8, 2026 in *The 74*, written by David Nurenberg, a high school English teacher. It is an abridged version of a peer-reviewed article forthcoming in Issue 115.6 of NCTE's *English Journal*. It describes Nurenberg's year-long AI literacy curriculum with 10th and 11th grade students.

The piece is the only source in this wiki that shows what AI literacy pedagogy looks like in actual secondary classroom practice — not theory, frameworks, or meta-analysis. For that reason it is especially useful as a bridge between the wiki's conceptual and evidence layers and the question of what teachers should concretely do.

## The Three-Option Frame

Nurenberg opens by rejecting two common responses to student AI use:

1. **Banning AI**: Nearly impossible to police; monitored in-class assessments privilege quick thinking over deep thinking and disadvantage neurodiverse and multilingual learners; no take-home assignment is fully "AI-proof."
2. **Freely allowing AI**: Removes the productive friction upon which learning depends and accelerates cognitive decline (Nurenberg cites the MIT EEG study — see [[cognitive-debt]]).

His "third option" — the middle path — is **teaching AI literacy using AI as the text itself**. Rather than positioning AI as a tool students use or are forbidden to use, his class studies AI as an object of critical inquiry while still using it instrumentally.

This maps onto the Brookings Institution and American Psychological Association recommendations he cites, and is operationally close to the "pedagogical friction" recommendation in [[illusion-of-understanding]].

## The Curriculum: AI as Object of Study

Nurenberg's students engaged with AI across five dimensions of their existing English class:

### 1. Literary Analysis
Students compared AI literary analysis of novels and short stories to their own interpretations. Finding: AI analysis was "generic" and "oversimplified," producing confident-sounding distillations that lacked the nuance in students' own "creative, discursive yet defensible readings." Students learned to distinguish actual analysis from simplistic summary — and to "suspect the allure of AI's instant 'correct answers.'"

This is a classroom-level replication of what [[illusion-of-understanding]] documents experimentally: AI fluency mimics quality, but students who develop the habit of comparison can learn to see past it.

### 2. Discussion
Students invited chatbots into class literary discussions. Students described the interactions as "bizarre" and "disjointed" — adequate for reviewing plot, but "too circular or directionless for genuinely provocative dialogue." ChatGPT's sycophancy was student-identified as the core problem:

> "One student 'started purposely saying dumb things just to see how GPT would still find a way to say "great idea." It just felt so fake.'"

This is a student-generated empirical encounter with [[ai-sycophancy]]: the trained deference that kills the productive tension real debate requires. The student did what a researcher would — ran a probe, got a diagnostic result, drew a conclusion. This is epistemic monitoring ([[agentivism]] P2) happening organically in a classroom.

### 3. Essay Writing
Students compared LLM-generated essays to their own. They identified AI prose as "sophisticated-sounding" yet "generic," contrasted with their own "messier" but more engaging writing. The takeaway Nurenberg hoped students internalized: in a world where everyone has LLM access, developing genuine voice is economically valuable, not just aesthetically preferable. ChatGPT should be "reserved for inter-office memos and letters to one's utility company."

The genuine voice finding has direct implications for [[transferable-skills]]: individual voice — the irreducibly personal dimension of writing — is exactly what AI cannot replicate and what makes human-produced work valuable in the [[relational-economy]].

### 4. Research and Source Evaluation
Students studied the structural limitations of AI-generated search summaries:
- Users are 58% less likely to look beyond AI search summaries (Ahrefs data)
- AI draws disproportionately from unregulated chat forums because paywalled scholarly research is inaccessible
- Sources often said something "very different than AI summaries claimed they did"
- Query wording ("is abortion safe" vs. "is abortion murder") generated politically slanted results

Students also studied the political economy of AI: who owns the models, how Google censors Gemini responses to political questions, algorithmic bias in image generation (requests for "doctor" returning mainly white males). The goal: move students past the assumption that AI tools are neutral utilities.

### 5. Note-Taking
Students compared manual note-taking to AI note-taking tool outputs, examining how choices about inclusion, emphasis, and phrasing propagate different narratives. The Africa-under-colonialism example: did colonialism "fuel worldwide industrial production" or were African peoples "exploited for the benefit of Western industrial profit"? AI note-taking naturalizes framing choices that are actually contested.

## The AI Audit: Replacing Bans with Accountability

The structural intervention Nurenberg describes is replacing AI bans with **AI audits**. For every AI tool students used, they had to demonstrate:

1. Knowledge of how the tool operates
2. What they felt they gained and lost by using it
3. How they verified the accuracy of information obtained
4. How they had not relinquished their own thinking

The audit does not conclude "AI is always bad." It requires students to demonstrate that AI use was deliberate, evaluated, and bounded — that the student remained the agent. This is a direct classroom operationalization of [[agentivism]]'s core mechanisms: selective delegation, epistemic monitoring, reconstructive internalization. The audit makes these mechanisms visible and mandatory rather than hoped-for.

## The Cognitive Surrender vs. Cognitive Offloading Distinction

Nurenberg cites a preprint distinguishing two modes of AI use (source: Why the Middle Path of AI Literacy May Be the Future of English Class.md):

- **Cognitive surrender**: passive trust and uncritical evaluation of external information — the student accepts AI output without evaluation
- **Cognitive offloading**: strategic delegation of specific cognition during deliberation — the student deliberately hands off a bounded task while retaining evaluative control

The AI literacy curriculum Nurenberg describes is designed to teach students to offload rather than surrender. This distinction maps onto [[agentivism]]'s contrast between assisted performance (cognitive surrender) and durable human capability growth (cognitive offloading with reconstruction).

It also parallels the [[illusion-of-understanding]] finding: students who exhibit cognitive surrender produce chance-level task outcomes while experiencing a feeling of competence. The audit is a structural intervention designed to make surrender visible and correctable.

**The term reaches federal policy discourse**: In June 2026, Erin Mote (CEO, InnovateEDU) used "cognitive surrender" in testimony before the Senate HELP subcommittee, describing consumer-grade AI's "algorithmic sycophancy" as producing exactly this pattern — citing a finding that users accepted incorrect chatbot answers 80% of the time. See [[senate-hearing-ai-k12-education]]. This is a notable trajectory for a term this page introduced to the wiki from a single classroom practitioner's account: within roughly a month of ingestion, the same concept and vocabulary appears in a US Senate hearing, suggesting genuine convergence between grassroots classroom practice and federal policy framing rather than the wiki simply tracking an already-established term.

## The Equity Tension

Nurenberg teaches an affluent population. He acknowledges that similar critical AI literacy approaches are being used with more economically and linguistically diverse learners — citing existing research — but his classroom account reflects the challenges that may be harder in under-resourced settings:

- **Less-confident students** never stopped viewing AI's "clear and well-organized" writing as superior to their own, and "still hesitated to trust their own readings of literature over 'the answers' ChatGPT offered." The protective factor — students trusting their own judgment enough to contest AI's — was weaker in students with lower academic confidence, consistent with the [[illusion-of-understanding]] finding that metacognition, not subject knowledge, is the key protective variable.
- **The multilingual student's story**: her father's dependence on her at a business meeting became a lens for understanding AI dependence — "even if AI help is readily available, perhaps we gain something by refusing to rely on it." Nurenberg notes this student made the connection herself; this is the reconstructive internalization [[agentivism]] describes.

## The Developmental Timing Argument

Nurenberg raises a pedagogical challenge he doesn't fully resolve: he struggles with asking students to critically evaluate AI while their own linguistic and analytic skills are still developing. But he concludes the alternative — waiting until students have mastered writing before introducing AI literacy — is not available:

> "I cannot create the conditions that allow teenagers to become master writers and thinkers before they are exposed to AI; they will soon arrive at my classroom having been using it since childhood."

He draws on post-pandemic learning science: when teaching anything, you cannot wait for below-grade-level students to catch up before introducing higher-order thinking skills. You must teach both simultaneously.

This connects to the [[ai-agents-k12-meta-analysis]] finding that high-school students (grades 10–12) show near-zero cognitive benefit from AI interventions. Nurenberg's curriculum does not deploy AI to improve content learning — it uses AI as the object of critical study, a different pedagogical design that sidesteps the grade-level effectiveness gap.

## Implications for the Wiki

The Nurenberg piece is the only practitioner-level account in the wiki of a sustained, implemented AI literacy curriculum at the secondary level. Its primary value is:

1. **Demonstrating that agentivism is teachable**: The AI audit is a concrete, classroom-ready operationalization of mechanisms that Yan & Gašević describe theoretically.
2. **Student-discovered sycophancy**: The "say dumb things" experiment is a powerful pedagogical example — students who run this probe have internalized a lasting heuristic about AI's trained deference.
3. **The cognitive surrender/offloading distinction**: This is the clearest student-facing language in the wiki for describing the difference between harmful and productive AI use.
4. **Genuine voice as a transferable skill**: The essay comparison exercise converts the abstract claim that AI can't replicate authentic personal voice into a direct student discovery.
