# Fostering AI-Ready Building & Moving CTE Pipelines

**Summary**: ERO/Advance CTE/ACTE report (March 2025) on AI's impact on the Building & Moving Career Cluster Grouping — Advanced Manufacturing, Construction, and Supply Chain & Transportation — finding these occupations are the *least* AI-exposed of the four cluster groupings studied in this report series.

**Sources**: `raw/PDF/Fostering-AI-Ready-Building-and-Moving-CTE-Pipelines.pdf`

**Last updated**: 2026-07-02

---

## About this report

By Cameron Sublett, Lauren Mason, Dani Rimbach-Jones, Karina Beltran, & Matias Fresard. Education Research & Opportunity Center (ERO), University of Tennessee Knoxville, in partnership with Advance CTE and ACTE, funded by ECMC Foundation. March 2025. The first in a four-part series applying the same [[ai-occupational-exposure-index]] methodology across four of Advance CTE's six modernized (Oct 2024) Career Cluster Groupings; see [[fostering-ai-ready-cultivating-resources]], [[fostering-ai-ready-caring-communities]], and [[fostering-ai-ready-creating-experiencing]] for the other three.

## What is the Building & Moving cluster grouping?

The Building & Moving grouping consists of three Career Clusters, employing **38,890,540 workers — nearly 26% of the total US workforce** (2023 BLS data) — making it the largest cluster grouping by employment in this report series:

- **Supply Chain & Transportation** (sub-clusters: Air & Space Transportation, Ground & Rail Transportation, Maintenance & Repair, Marine Transportation, Planning & Logistics, Purchasing & Warehousing) — occupations include automobile service technician, air traffic controller, urban and regional manager, transportation inspector
- **Advanced Manufacturing** (sub-clusters: Engineering, Industrial Machinery, Production & Automation, Robotics, Safety & Quality Assurance) — occupations include engineer, industrial machinery mechanic, chemical plant and system operator, occupational health and safety technician
- **Construction** (sub-clusters: Architecture & Civil Engineering, Construction Planning & Development, Equipment Operation & Maintenance, Skilled Trades) — occupations include architectural and civil drafter, carpenter, welder, urban and regional planner

## How AI is revolutionizing these occupations

**Supply Chain & Transportation**: AI optimizes air traffic routing around weather and congestion; Waymo has expanded driverless ride-hailing fleets across Los Angeles and Phoenix, accumulating millions of miles of driving data; AI-driven traffic analysis and rail surveillance improve safety and scheduling; the Delaware Department of Transportation is building an AI-powered predictive Integrated Transportation Management System; Amazon and Walmart use predictive ML for routing and demand forecasting, with documented cost reductions (source: Fostering-AI-Ready-Building-and-Moving-CTE-Pipelines.pdf, citing McKinsey 2021/2024).

Nick Goodnight, an automotive and diesel technologies professor at Ivy Tech Community College (Indiana), told the report's authors he is confident AI will not eliminate the majority of Building & Moving occupations. He described AI's main effect as improving communication "between vehicle and technician, and the communication between the vehicle and the built environment," and — pointing to faster cellular networks reducing processing latency — said "a wave is forming" in the automotive industry "and we are at the top of the wave."

**Advanced Manufacturing**: AI computer vision performs product inspection and defect detection; generative AI accelerates design iteration in tools like AutoCAD and Siemens NX; VR/AR integration (Industry 4.0) is documented to enhance maintenance operations by up to 79% and reduce errors by up to 92% (Fiorentino et al., 2014); machine learning models predict safety risks from historical data.

Paul Thurber, VP and GM of manufacturing and safety for Everguard.ai, told the authors that AI won't change the skills workers need or eliminate the need for training — instead it functions "as a proverbial tap on the shoulder, saying 'hey, put on your hat,' 'tie off here,' 'don't walk there,' 'that equipment is energized.'" He described it as a "brother's keeper," watching out for worker safety "24/7, 365, without distraction or interruption," with measurable reductions in safety incidents.

Tim Sisson of Trane Technologies described using AI-driven computer modeling to create "virtual twins" — exact virtual replicas of physical HVAC systems used to simulate earthquakes and other failure conditions before manufacturing, helping ensure systems like hospital air circulation continue functioning under stress.

**Construction**: Boston Dynamics' Spot robot autonomously navigates sites, conducts inspections, and collects data; AI-driven simulations train workers on safety scenarios; AI-powered exoskeletons and wearables monitor vital signs, fatigue, and environmental risk in real time; drones conduct structural assessments; AI-powered robotics handle repetitive tasks like bricklaying and concrete pouring with greater precision and lower labor cost; generative site design optimizes site selection using geospatial data.

## Will AI automate Building & Moving occupations?

Applying the [[ai-occupational-exposure-index]] methodology, the report finds Building & Moving AIOE scores are **generally negative — below the national average** — meaning these occupations are less exposed to AI than the typical US occupation. This is the most favorable exposure profile of the four cluster groupings in this report series.

The exceptions are the "high-skill" occupations with higher entry-education requirements: **Industrial Engineers (AIOE 1.14), Mechanical Engineers (AIOE 1.00), Buyers and Purchasing Agents (AIOE 0.85), Civil Engineers (AIOE 0.85), and Industrial Production Managers (AIOE 0.51)** all score above the national mean, driven by their higher share of cognitive tasks. By contrast, most hands-on trade occupations score well below zero: **Laborers and freight/stock/material movers (AIOE −1.71), Landscaping and groundskeeping workers, Industrial truck and tractor operators (−1.40), Welders/cutters/solderers/brazers (−1.20), Industrial machinery mechanics (−1.13), Heavy and tractor-trailer truck drivers (−1.15)**.

Across the three clusters, Advanced Manufacturing's average AIOE sits just slightly above the overall national average, while Construction and Supply Chain & Transportation both roughly mirror the national average. As with the other reports in this series, the finding is explicitly not a displacement forecast: "where there are higher AIOE scores, there is a higher likelihood that AI will be integrated into those fields of work" — for bachelor's-level Building & Moving roles, that integration is framed as enhancement of human labor, not replacement.

## Recommendations

- **CTE providers — shift the narrative**: AI exposure cannot be optional coursework; every Building & Moving program should train students on AI applications in their field. The report cites evidence that colleges serving more affluent students are more likely to offer AI training than open-access community colleges (Palmer, 2025), and quotes NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang: "You are not going to lose your job to AI, you're going to lose your job to someone who uses AI."
- **Focus on skills, not just jobs**: technology acts on tasks and skills, not occupations directly (Acemoglu & Autor, 2011); credential design organized around majors and degrees does students a disservice relative to skills-based design.
- **Industry partners**: should share concrete AI use cases and equip classrooms with current AI-driven tools; the report notes that industry itself is often "unsure of the AI landscape," with community college deans sometimes serving as the de facto authority on AI at industry meetings.
- **Policymakers**: should build on the Perkins V Comprehensive Local Needs Assessment as an existing vehicle for employer-education AI alignment, fund apprenticeships and work-based learning, and continue investment given equipment costs (one dean estimated over $1M to establish a modern automotive technologies program).

## Conclusion

The report frames Gen Z's growing interest in trades-based work as the **"toolbelt generation"** (a term from Te-Ping Chen's April 2024 *Wall Street Journal* article, "How Gen Z Is Becoming the Toolbelt Generation"), and concludes this demographic shift will buoy an already large and AI-resistant Building & Moving workforce. AI will substitute some routine tasks and may fully automate a small number of roles, but the report's central finding is that the vast majority of Building & Moving occupations will persist, with workers combining technical, transferable, and AI literacy skills positioned to thrive.
