# AI Labor Disruption Segments

**Summary**: BCG Henderson Institute's six-category framework for classifying all US jobs by their exposure to AI — the most granular occupational taxonomy currently available, distinguishing between roles that will be amplified, rebalanced, diverged, substituted, enabled, or left with limited AI exposure.

**Sources**: `raw/PDF/ai-will-reshape-more-jobs-than-it-replaces.pdf`

**Last updated**: 2026-05-13

---

## Overview

The BCG six-segment framework addresses a fundamental weakness in prior AI labor market analysis: binary "at risk / not at risk" categorizations that conflate substitution with augmentation, and ignore demand-side dynamics. The framework applies three sequential factors to classify every occupational role:

1. **Task automation potential**: What share of the role's tasks can current AI automate?
2. **Augmentation vs. substitution**: Does AI replace human work or extend it? (Determined by: human interaction intensity, judgment requirements, and process structure)
3. **Demand expandability**: If productivity rises, does demand expand to absorb it? (Determined by: unmet demand headroom and structural scalability)

The result is six distinct segments, not a spectrum. A role's landing point depends on the *combination* of these factors, not the magnitude of any single one. (source: ai-will-reshape-more-jobs-than-it-replaces.pdf)

## The Six Segments

### Amplified (5%)

**Characteristics**: High automation potential + augmented (not substituted) + expandable demand

**What happens**: AI dramatically increases individual productivity; demand expands to absorb gains; headcount holds or grows despite efficiency improvement.

**Canonical example**: Software engineers. Coding AI doubled output per engineer; cost per software feature fell; businesses purchased more software; engineering headcount increased post-ChatGPT. The Jevons Paradox operating as theory predicts.

**Worker experience**: Highest upside. Skills compound with AI. "AI fluency" becomes a force multiplier for already-skilled workers.

**CTE relevance**: Few CTE-adjacent roles fall here, but the Amplified dynamic is what the [[applied-co-intelligence-model]] aims to produce within occupational context — workers who use AI to expand their output, not just automate tasks.

---

### Rebalanced (14%)

**Characteristics**: Mixed automation potential + augmented + expandable demand

**What happens**: AI automates some tasks within the role while creating new tasks (managing AI outputs, handling escalations, building prompts). The role changes composition substantially but headcount is relatively stable.

**Canonical examples**: Financial advisors (AI handles portfolio screening; advisor handles relationship management, complex planning); some physician specialties (AI handles imaging review; physician handles diagnosis communication and care coordination).

**Worker experience**: Role transformation is real and requires active adaptation. Workers who adapt gain leverage; workers who don't face skill obsolescence within an otherwise stable headcount environment.

**CTE relevance**: Many healthcare and business services CTE pathways lead here. This segment argues for embedding AI adaptation skills into CTE curricula — not as a standalone AI literacy module, but as part of the occupational competency itself.

---

### Divergent (12%)

**Characteristics**: High automation potential + partially augmented + expandable demand — *but* automation hits entry-level tasks disproportionately

**What happens**: AI automates the junior and routine tasks within the role; senior/complex functions persist or grow. Entry-level positions contract; senior positions hold or expand. The pipeline that creates senior workers through junior experience is disrupted.

**Canonical examples**: Junior consultants, entry-level analysts, early-career legal associates. The tasks that formed career on-ramps (document review, basic research, data formatting) are automated first.

**Worker experience**: Bifurcated. Experienced workers are largely protected. Entry-level workers face a hiring squeeze — their starting positions are the ones AI targets. "AI fluency may become an increasingly important complement to tenure." (source: ai-will-reshape-more-jobs-than-it-replaces.pdf)

**CTE relevance**: This is the mechanism behind the [[anthropic-labor-market-index]] finding of a 14% hiring slowdown among young workers. It is also the segment that creates the most direct near-term risk for young people entering professional fields from CTE or college pathways. Portfolio work, project-based demonstration of senior-level judgment, and AI-augmented work samples may substitute partially for on-the-job junior experience.

---

### Substituted (12%)

**Characteristics**: High automation potential + substituted (not augmented) + bounded demand

**What happens**: AI replaces the core function of the role; demand does not expand to compensate; headcount contracts. This is the displacement scenario.

**Canonical examples**: Call center customer service representatives, routine data entry clerks, document processing workers.

**Worker experience**: Highest displacement risk. The role's core tasks are exactly what AI does well, and customers or employers do not value the human element enough to pay a premium for it.

**CTE relevance**: CTE programs that prepare students primarily for Substituted roles need redesign. The [[applied-co-intelligence-report]] would classify these as roles where "technical skills alone" without AI mastery will not provide durable employment. Workers in these roles should be targeted for reskilling toward Enabled or Limited-Exposure functions.

---

### Enabled (23%)

**Characteristics**: Low to moderate automation potential + augmented + various demand

**What happens**: AI gives these workers new capabilities — better information, decision support, administrative automation — without threatening the core function that requires physical presence, human interaction, or contextual judgment.

**Canonical examples**: Nurses (AI assists documentation and medication review; the physical care function is not automated), social workers, skilled tradespeople.

**Worker experience**: Net positive if AI adoption is managed well. Workers gain leverage without facing displacement. The primary risk is that organizations capture productivity gains through workload increases rather than investing in worker flourishing or headcount growth.

**CTE relevance**: The largest single segment relevant to CTE programs. Healthcare, skilled trades, early childhood education, and social services all fall here. BCG's framework confirms that these roles are structurally insulated from substitution while gaining AI capability. This supports [[cte-and-ai]]'s argument that CTE sectors have a distinct advantage in navigating AI disruption.

---

### Limited-Exposure (34%)

**Characteristics**: Low automation potential (<25% of tasks) — augmentation vs. substitution question does not arise

**What happens**: Current AI cannot meaningfully automate the core tasks. Work is defined by physical presence, fine motor dexterity, sustained personal relationship, or real-time contextual adaptation in variable physical environments.

**Canonical examples**: Physicians (physical examination and relationship-intensive care), elementary school teachers, construction workers, personal care aides.

**Worker experience**: Largely insulated from displacement by current AI. The primary AI impact is administrative (documentation, scheduling) rather than core-function displacement. This may change as physical AI/robotics advance — a limitation BCG explicitly notes.

**CTE relevance**: The single most important segment for CTE policy. 34% of all US jobs — approximately 56 million workers — fall here. The BCG data provides the most rigorous quantitative confirmation of the [[relational-economy]] thesis: that structural change will expand employment in precisely the domains that require embodied human presence. The sectors CTE trains — healthcare, hospitality, construction, early childhood — are the core of this 34%.

---

## Combined Segments: The Optimistic 57%

Critically: Limited-Exposure (34%) + Enabled (23%) = **57% of US jobs** are either insulated from AI displacement or gain net capability from AI without elimination risk. This is substantially more optimistic than the Frey & Osborne (2013) estimate of 47% at high risk, which did not apply demand-side analysis. (source: ai-will-reshape-more-jobs-than-it-replaces.pdf)

The remaining 43% split into:
- Amplified (5%): Headcount may grow
- Rebalanced (14%): Roles change, headcount relatively stable
- Divergent (12%): Entry-level shrinks, senior holds
- Substituted (12%): Genuine headcount contraction

So the realistic displacement population is closer to 12% (Substituted) with a pipeline problem in another 12% (Divergent) — not 47%.

## Connection to Demand Expandability

The segment a role lands in is not determined solely by automation potential. Two roles can have identical automation exposure and diverge into Amplified vs. Substituted depending entirely on demand expandability:

- Software engineering (high automation, expandable demand) → Amplified
- Call center work (high automation, bounded demand) → Substituted

This is why BCG's framework is more useful for workforce planning than automation-potential-only analyses. See [[bcg-ai-reshapes-jobs]] for the full methodology. The Jevons Paradox connection is analyzed in [[apocalypse-no-galloway]] and [[ai-job-apocalypse-klein]].
