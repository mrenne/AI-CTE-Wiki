# AI Mastery Continuum

**Summary**: A four-level developmental framework from the Applied Co-Intelligence report for tracking a CTE learner's progression from basic AI awareness to the ability to critique, adapt, and innovate with AI tools within an occupational context.

**Sources**: `raw/PDF/Applied-Co-Intelligence–Preparing-Career-and-Technical-Education-Learners-for-an-AI-Driven-Workforce-FINAL.pdf`

**Last updated**: 2026-04-25

---

## Overview

The AI Mastery Continuum is one of the three spheres of the [[applied-co-intelligence-model]]. It describes how a CTE learner progresses from generic AI awareness to occupation-specific AI command. The continuum has two dimensions: the four stages (X-axis) and depth of application from Generic to Systems-Level (Y-axis). As a learner advances along the stages, their ability to apply AI in meaningful, job-specific ways deepens.

The continuum is presented as a theoretical framework intended for researchers to test and refine, not a fixed standard (source: Applied-Co-Intelligence PDF).

## The Four Levels

### AI Literacy
**Definition**: Broad awareness of AI tools, capabilities, limitations, and ethical considerations.

The baseline — often achieved through general education or independent exploration. Not yet tethered to occupational context. For CTE educators, AI Literacy is the **starting point**, not the destination.

*Examples*: A nursing student prompts an LLM to create a cardiovascular pharmacology quiz. A welding student asks ChatGPT to explain MIG vs. TIG welding for exam prep. A marketing student asks ChatGPT to write an ad and uses the output directly without adapting it for brand or audience.

### AI Fluency
**Definition**: The ability to effectively integrate specific AI tools into established occupational workflows with competence and consistency.

This level is the **first outcome of intentional pedagogy**. Educators design tasks requiring learners to integrate AI into their specific workflow. Learners shift from passive exploration to targeted application. Fluency is marked by critical acumen — the ability to discern information quality and troubleshoot flaws in AI outputs.

*Examples*: A supply chain student explores a specialized logistics platform (SAP IBP) and understands how its predictive algorithms differ from spreadsheets. A medical assistant student learns an AI-enabled EHR system's ambient listening features for clinical documentation.

### AI Agency
**Definition**: Confident, proactive contextual selection, configuration, and application of AI tools — requiring judgment, adaptability, and ownership.

Educators design complex, open-ended "messy" problems where learners must choose the correct tool and strategy rather than being told which to use. Learners understand the underlying mechanics of AI tools and begin shaping how AI is used in their occupational domain.

*Examples*: An automotive technology student selects a specific AI diagnostic platform, calibrates settings for a vehicle type, and interprets results to troubleshoot engine performance. A CNA student evaluates three AI diagnostic tools for a high-stakes simulation, justifying her choice based on HIPAA compliance and data security.

### AI Mastery
**Definition**: Deep understanding of AI systems and their implications — the ability to critique, adapt, and innovate with AI tools.

Educators become mentors and capstone facilitators, challenging learners to critique systems or build new solutions. Mastery is not just technical; it is reflective, ethical, and strategic. Positions learners as contributors to AI's responsible development within their industries.

*Examples*: A cybersecurity student customizes AI-driven threat detection algorithms, evaluates their effectiveness across network environments, and proposes improvements to reduce false positives. A CNA student identifies biases in her simulation lab's predictive algorithm and builds a RAG-enabled LLM to give fellow students access to clinical protocols.

## Educator Roles at Each Level

| Level | Educator's Role |
|-------|----------------|
| Literacy | Hands-off; allows exploration |
| Fluency | Integration coach |
| Agency | Designer of complex, open-ended scenarios |
| Mastery | Mentor, capstone facilitator, research guide |

## Relationship to Agentivism

The continuum maps directly onto [[agentivism]]'s mechanisms:

- **Fluency** → epistemic monitoring begins; learner critically audits AI outputs
- **Agency** → selective delegation; learner retains responsibility for problem framing
- **Mastery** → reconstructive internalization; transfer under reduced support

The key insight shared by both frameworks: passive AI use (Literacy) produces no durable learning. Intentional pedagogy is required to move learners up the continuum.

## Stand-Alone vs. Integrated Delivery

The report argues against stand-alone "AI 101" courses in favor of embedding AI skills within existing technical and capstone courses. The reasoning (source: Applied-Co-Intelligence PDF):

1. AI use cases vary by CTE cluster — generative AI dominates Arts/Design/Marketing while machine learning and sensor technology dominate Agriculture and Construction
2. Domain expertise is what separates a novice AI user from an expert co-intelligent partner — an automotive student can judge an AI diagnostic tool only by comparing its output against their own technical knowledge
