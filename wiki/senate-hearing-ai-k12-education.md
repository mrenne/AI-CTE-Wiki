## The Witnesses

- **Joshua Jones**, CEO of QuantHub (Birmingham, AL), which builds AI/data literacy curriculum for Alabama's K-12 and postsecondary systems in partnership with the state Department of Education.
- **Erin Mote**, CEO of InnovateEDU, founder of Project Unicorn, and leader of the Ed Safe AI Alliance — a charter school founder and self-described "technologist" with two decades at the intersection of technology, policy, and systemic change.

## Core Findings

### No Causal Evidence on Long-Term Effects

Erin Mote, under direct questioning from Sen. Tuberville: "there is currently no high-quality causal studies on the long-term effects of A.I. on student learning, equity, or social emotional development... What we have is a number of studies that are short-term and tool based." This independently corroborates [[evidence-base-ai-k12]]'s Stanford SCALE finding (818 papers reviewed, only 20 causal, zero high-quality US K-12 student studies) — via congressional testimony rather than academic synthesis, adding a second, differently-sourced confirmation of the same evidence gap. (source: Senate Hearing on Artificial Intelligence and K-12 Education.md)

### Cognitive Surrender

Mote's central framing: consumer-grade large language models are "engineered for platform retention and user satisfaction," and default to "a dangerous form of algorithmic sycophancy — it does not teach, it indulges, it validates incorrect premises and hand-delivers answers, often bypassing the productive struggle required for cognitive development." She cites a finding (attributed by Sen. Murphy to "Professor Warren") that when a chatbot gave an incorrect answer, users accepted the error 80% of the time. Mote's term "cognitive surrender" is the same term used in [[middle-path-ai-literacy-nurenberg]] to distinguish deliberate, monitored delegation (cognitive offloading, potentially fine) from unmonitored capitulation to AI's answers (cognitive surrender, harmful) — this hearing shows the term has entered federal policy discourse, not just K-12 practitioner writing.

### The Neuroscience/Age Argument for Differentiated Regulation

Sen. Chris Murphy, referencing "Professor Warren['s]" research on cognitive surrender, argued that "there is a really big difference in putting this technology in front of a brain that is developing" versus an adult brain, proposing (in the style of car-rental age restrictions) a differentiated regulatory approach for AI companions with a rough 25-and-under threshold, on the premise that neural pathway development is substantially complete by that age. He also cited quantified figures — human-only tutoring producing a 4.5-percentage-point learning gain versus 10 percentage points for AI providing "just-in-time diagnostic" hints under human judgment — without a named published source; this claim should be treated as unverified testimony, not an established citable finding, until sourced independently.

## State Policy Case Studies

### Delaware's AI Assurance Lab (Cindy Marten)

Delaware's approach centers on independent, pre-adoption testing rather than trusting vendor claims: "Vision has to come before vendor." Trained educators, administrators, technical experts, and Department of Education staff test AI tools in real Delaware classroom scenarios against national frameworks and the state's own guidance, evaluating accuracy, appropriateness, bias, and accessibility before districts adopt at scale. Delaware's 2024 statewide generative-AI guidance is grounded in four priorities: protect student data, ensure ethical use, promote equity, and enhance teaching and learning. The state's strategic-plan goal is 75% of districts and charter schools using AI tools while maintaining full compliance with data security, privacy, and "human in the loop" requirements. Marten's three-step "Empowering Leaders Toolkit" roadmap: (1) mitigate risk, (2) build strategy around the instructional core, (3) guide and evaluate use over time. (source: Senate Hearing on Artificial Intelligence and K-12 Education.md)

### Alabama's Data Scholars Program (Joshua Jones)

QuantHub's four-year partnership with the Alabama Department of Education ranks Alabama third nationally in student data-science course enrollment (per the National Data Science for Everyone State of the Field report), behind only California and Virginia, and first nationally in students earning industry-recognized data/AI credentials before graduation. The program's Data Scholars initiative has placed 100+ high school students into paid AI/data internships across 50+ employers — a scale Jones calls unusually high for work-based learning recruitment, and therefore itself "a strong signal that this talent is in real demand."

Jones's key structural insight: **curriculum refresh cycles must match AI's pace of change, not the traditional textbook cycle.** "The traditional curriculum refresh rate is typically five to ten years, and in the age of AI, that is generations." QuantHub's response is a human-in-the-loop system — AI agents generate curriculum drafts, but instructional designers and subject-matter experts validate everything before it reaches a student — reducing development time from roughly 200 hours to about 10 hours of validation per learning hour, a 20x improvement Jones describes as achieved "without compromising rigor, efficacy, or accuracy." His federal policy recommendations: fund curriculum infrastructure that updates continuously (semester cadence, not decade cadence); weight implementation/training as heavily as content creation in federal workforce-education grants; expand paid work-based learning in AI/data fields starting in high school. (source: Senate Hearing on Artificial Intelligence and K-12 Education.md)

## Data Privacy and Security

- **The PowerSchool breach**: exposed data belonging to 62 million students and 9.5 million teachers — described in the hearing as the largest breach of children's education data in US history.
- **50%+ of US school districts experienced a cybersecurity incident in 2025** (per Sen. Blunt Rochester's opening statement).
- Mote's assessment: existing educational-privacy law functions reasonably well for purpose-built education technology, but the gap is **consumer-grade tools entering classrooms** (general chatbots, AI companions) that are not necessarily subject to the same restrictions — and where student data harvesting may occur without district or family awareness.
- The average school district accesses nearly 3,000 digital tools per year, creating what Mote calls "a fragmented, unmanageable landscape" that evidence-based procurement standards are meant to address.

## Proposed Evaluation Frameworks

Mote proposed several concrete, reusable evaluation heuristics:

- **Five Tech Quality Indicators** for procurement: tools must be safe, interoperable, usable, inclusive, and evidence-based. Her claim: fewer than half of purpose-built education-technology tools currently meet this evidence-based standard, and only 2% of consumer-grade tools do.
- **"Return on Instruction" (ROI)**, replacing simple usage/engagement metrics: does the tool measurably help an educator teach better or a student learn more?
- **"Screen value" over "screen time"**: raw usage-minute counts don't indicate whether an interaction is pedagogically meaningful. Mote cited a claim that screen-based reading is 6–8x less effective for reading comprehension than physical books, and that reading fluency requires roughly 1,000 practice repetitions ("at-bats") — both offered without an academic citation in the hearing and flagged here as needing independent verification.
- **A "waterfall" regulatory approach**: apply the heaviest safety controls to the highest-risk applications (public consumer AI platforms, "relationship simulating" companion chatbots) at the top of the funnel, while reducing regulatory burden on lower-risk, purpose-built educational tools — an explicit alternative to blanket bans or uniform screen-time limits, which Mote argues disproportionately harm students with disabilities who rely on digital tools for mandated accommodations.

## Civil Rights and Equity Concerns

Mote, citing advocacy groups (ACLU, EdTrust, Center for Democracy and Technology), named four specific harm categories under active civil-rights advocacy scrutiny:

1. **Bias in AI grading/detection tools**
2. **Discriminatory predictive analytics** — her example: a student using the word "y'all" in writing being algorithmically classified into "a lower cognitive category," illustrating dialect-based bias in automated assessment
3. **Student privacy and surveillance** — how collected data is stored and used
4. **Digital divide risk** — rural and under-resourced schools lacking access to the same AI tools as well-resourced schools, deepening rather than narrowing existing achievement gaps

Florida was cited as a leading state example on the legislative response side, having passed an AI Civil Rights Bill establishing "consequential decision-making" requirements — mandating a human reviewer whenever AI contributes to a decision such as a loan, college admission, financial aid award, or insurance claim.

## Legislative Context

- **The RAYS Act** (referenced by Blunt Rochester and Husted as bill they co-sponsor) would require *states*, not the federal government, to establish statewide standards for AI use in the classroom — reflecting a consistent theme across all three witnesses that federal guidance and evidence-building should support, not supplant, state and local decision-making authority.
- The hearing opened with a sharply partisan note: Sen. Blunt Rochester objected to the Department of Education's announced plan (under Secretary McMahon) to transfer core departmental functions, including civil-rights enforcement, to other agencies without Congressional approval — establishing contentious political context around federal education-technology capacity that is separate from, but adjacent to, the AI-specific testimony.

## Implications for CTE

1. **Alabama's Data Scholars model is a directly replicable CTE workforce-pipeline pattern** — paid internships tied to state curriculum standards, industry-recognized credentials earned before graduation, and continuous employer feedback loops keeping curriculum aligned with actual labor demand. This is a concrete implementation example for the abstractions in [[applied-co-intelligence-model]] and [[cte-futures-aci-webinar]].
2. **The curriculum-refresh-cadence problem is a CTE-specific structural challenge.** CTE curriculum, tied to industry-recognized credentials and employer-validated skill standards, faces the same "five-to-ten-year refresh cycle in a semester-cycle world" problem Jones describes — arguably more acutely than general academic curriculum, since CTE credentials must track live employer skill demand.
3. **Federal funding mechanisms (Perkins V, Title II, Title IV) were repeatedly named across witnesses** as the existing levers for supporting AI-literacy professional development and workforce pipeline programs — reinforcing the [[cte-futures-aci-webinar]] argument that CLNA and existing Perkins V mechanisms, not new standalone AI legislation, are the practical implementation path.
