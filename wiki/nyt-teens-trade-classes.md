# NYT: These Teens Are Choosing Trade Classes to AI-Proof Their Futures

**Summary**: A June 2026 New York Times video report documenting Bay Area high school seniors explicitly choosing trades as an AI hedge — the first primary journalistic source in the wiki showing student-level behavioral response to AI anxiety in the class of 2026.

## Overview

Published June 3, 2026, this short-form NYT video (~4 minutes) follows graduating seniors at a California public high school with a construction trades program. The location is significant: the Bay Area — the self-described "AI capital" of the world — is the setting for students who have watched the tech industry restructure in real time and are explicitly recalculating their career paths in response.

The piece is not an economic analysis or a policy report. It is ground-level primary evidence: students in their own words articulating why they are choosing trades, teachers describing what CTE programs can and cannot do, and parents illustrating the cultural friction that persists despite the wage and AI-resilience arguments. (source: These Teens Are Choosing Trade Classes to AI-Proof Their Futures.md)

---

## The AI Hedge Argument — From Students, Not Researchers

The most striking feature of the video is how fluently students articulate the AI-insulation logic that researchers have only recently formalized:

> "A.I. is not going to build a home. A.I. isn't going to weld anything either."

> "I don't have any friends that are really wanting to go into the tech industry at all because of A.I."

> "I've heard a lot about coders — how A.I. just completely eliminated them."

This is the [[bcg-ai-reshapes-jobs]] Limited-Exposure and [[broken-ladder]] double-insulation thesis arriving at street level — electricians and construction workers at the bottom of both WFH and GenAI exposure rankings, as students independently reason their way to the same conclusion. One student explicitly pivoted from pre-med to electrician after the career calculus shifted.

The framing is defensive, not aspirational: "If your plan is not future-proof, the world is going to eat you." Students are not enthusiastic about trades per se — they are performing risk arbitrage across what they perceive as a landscape where no path is safe. (source: These Teens Are Choosing Trade Classes to AI-Proof Their Futures.md)

## CTE as a Discovery Pathway

A critical structural finding from the teacher's testimony:

> "I would say over 95 percent of my students, maybe even higher, have never used any tools before in their life. And I would say almost 100 percent had never used a power tool."

And from a student:

> "I had no idea this class existed. I didn't even really consider construction seriously until I took this class."

This documents what the [[applied-co-intelligence-report]] and [[cte-futures-aci-webinar]] implicitly assume but rarely state directly: **CTE programs are the discovery mechanism.** Students cannot choose pathways they have not been exposed to. High school CTE programs — particularly those with active, hands-on curricula — are the point of first contact that makes trade careers a live option for students who would otherwise default to a four-year degree path.

The policy implication: California's decision to double its construction trades grant in 2021 is not just a supply response to a labor shortage — it is the structural precondition for students having trade careers as a choice set at all. Without funded exposure, the choice cannot be made. (source: These Teens Are Choosing Trade Classes to AI-Proof Their Futures.md)

## The College ROI Collapse

The video documents the explicit erosion of the "go to college, get a degree, make money" narrative among this cohort:

> "No one cares how much you know because ChatGPT knows more."

> "You're paying $400,000 and then what are you going to get from it?"

> "There's so many people with multiple degrees that are broke."

This student-level skepticism aligns with the [[columbia-cs-ai-disruption]] finding that CS enrollment is falling and the [[stanford-ai-index-education-2026]] data showing that 80% of students use AI for schoolwork without any institutional AI literacy framework. Students have integrated AI into their academic practice — and some have drawn the logical conclusion that academic credentials built around knowledge retrieval face a compressed return on investment.

The six-figure wage narrative is arriving through social media channels: "I became a millionaire from construction like a year and a half ago." YouTube and TikTok trades content is directly competing with traditional college-path messaging and winning with a measurable share of this cohort. (source: These Teens Are Choosing Trade Classes to AI-Proof Their Futures.md)

## Trade Stigma: The Persistent Barrier

The video is candid about the cultural friction that limits the trade pivot:

> "A lot of people, especially older generations, still believe that trades are like dirty."

> "I come from an immigrant family, and so pressure has been even more heavy. They're supportive. They're just a little disappointed that I'm not going to college."

> "I have students who are very good carpenters, and their parents still want them to go to college, and I totally respect that."

The teacher notes parents resist even for students who have demonstrated genuine aptitude — "They think it is less than." This is the structural demand-side constraint on CTE enrollment that no policy investment fully resolves. State grants can fund programs; they cannot override family cultural capital expectations that four-year degrees signal status and social mobility.

For CTE institutions, the trade stigma finding means that the challenge is not only building programs but reaching the parents and families who shape student choices before high school enrollment decisions are made. The [[applied-co-intelligence-report]] framing of CTE as preparation for a relational, human-essential economy — rather than as a fallback for students who didn't make it to college — is directly relevant here. (source: These Teens Are Choosing Trade Classes to AI-Proof Their Futures.md)

## California Policy Context

California is the explicit backdrop:

- **2021 grant doubling**: The state doubled a construction trades grant to address a documented construction labor shortage — not primarily an AI response, but an infrastructure investment that created the programs these students are now choosing for AI-hedge reasons
- **Labor market pull**: A construction labor shortage precedes the AI anxiety; students can earn $200,000 per year as electricians; this wage premium makes the risk arbitrage calculus easier
- **Geographic irony**: The Bay Area — home to the tech companies restructuring white-collar employment — is where students are most directly observing AI's effect on professional career paths and making the most immediate trade pivot

This provides narrative grounding for the causal IV finding in [[nber-data-centers-local-economies]]: AI infrastructure investment creates real construction trade demand, and in the AI capital itself, the students who will fill that pipeline are actively recalculating toward it. (source: These Teens Are Choosing Trade Classes to AI-Proof Their Futures.md)

## The Commencement Backlash: A Parallel 2026 Signal

[[brad-smith-ai-jobs-next-generation]] (Smith, June 2026) documents a complementary behavioral signal from the same graduating cohort. At university commencement ceremonies across the United States, the class of 2026 booed AI when it was mentioned from the podium. At Princeton, students petitioned to reject a beer jacket design created with AI assistance, replacing it with one labeled "100% cotton — 100% human."

Smith — the President of Microsoft — frames this as a "wake-up call" that tech sector leaders "had better take notice" of. His observation: these students are also, according to Microsoft's own research, the *highest AI adopters* in the country (counties with large college-age populations show the highest generative AI usage rates in the US). When a generation's most enthusiastic adopters are also its loudest critics, the signal carries extra weight.

The commencement backlash and the Bay Area trade pivot documented in this source share the same psychological root: both reflect a generation that has processed AI's impact in real time and is asserting the value of distinctly human work — whether through trade enrollment or through the explicit rejection of AI-generated creative artifacts. The Princeton jacket's "100% human" label is the same logic as "A.I. is not going to build a home" — expressed in the register of self-definition rather than career arbitrage. (source: AI, jobs, and the next generation.md)

## Corporate Validation: Zuckerberg Confirms the Demand

Just days after this report was published, the CEO of one of the largest AI infrastructure spenders explicitly confirmed the demand the Bay Area students were betting on:

> "America is going to need hundreds of thousands of skilled tradespeople to build the infrastructure for our country to lead in AI." — Mark Zuckerberg, June 9, 2026

Meta simultaneously announced $115 million for "America's Workforce Academy" — free data center construction training with NCCER credentials, launching in Louisiana, Ohio, Indiana, and Texas in 2026. The program's predecessor (Level-Up fiber installation training) attracted 35,000 applications in its first week ([[meta-workforce-academy]]).

The Bay Area students making their trade pivot in June 2026 are doing so in the same moment that the CEO of a $600B-infrastructure-committing tech company is explicitly naming "hundreds of thousands of skilled tradespeople" as the workforce need. Student risk arbitrage and executive labor demand forecasts have converged on the same answer. (source: Mark Zuckerberg Says America Needs...md)

## "No Path Is Safe": The Psychological Frame

The underlying affect of this cohort is not confidence in trades as a safe harbor — it is generalized anxiety about all paths:

> "This generation, especially, you really have to think about what you're going to do. Because if your plan is not future-proof, the world is going to eat you."

> "It feels as though the economy is like just continuously moving against us."

These students grew up in the pandemic, watched the tech industry restructure, and are graduating into an economy they perceive as unstable in every direction. The trade pivot is not an expression of enthusiasm for skilled craftsmanship — it is a calculated bet on physical-presence insulation in a landscape where no credential feels durable.

This psychological framing is structurally consistent with the [[sinead-bovell-job-market]] independence era argument: the organize-around-skills-not-job-titles insight is not aspirational for this generation, it is a survival response. It also connects to [[ai-job-apocalypse-klein]]'s concern about relational skills atrophying — young people calibrating for economic risk in an atmosphere of pervasive uncertainty. (source: These Teens Are Choosing Trade Classes to AI-Proof Their Futures.md)
