# Fostering AI-Ready Caring for Communities CTE Pipelines

**Summary**: ERO/Advance CTE/ACTE report (June 2025) on AI's impact on the Caring for Communities Career Cluster Grouping — Education, Healthcare & Human Services, and Public Service & Safety — finding this grouping has the *highest* AI exposure of the four studied, driven by teachers, medical/health managers, and lawyers.

**Last updated**: 2026-07-02

## About this report

By Dani Rimbach-Jones, Cameron Sublett, Lauren Mason, & Matias Fresard. Education Research & Opportunity Center (ERO), University of Tennessee Knoxville, in partnership with Advance CTE and ACTE, funded by ECMC Foundation. June 2025. Part of the four-part series; see [[fostering-ai-ready-building-moving]], [[fostering-ai-ready-cultivating-resources]], and [[fostering-ai-ready-creating-experiencing]] for the other three, and [[ai-occupational-exposure-index]] for the shared methodology.

## What is the Caring for Communities cluster grouping?

The Caring for Communities grouping consists of three Career Clusters:

- **Education** (sub-clusters: Early Childhood Development, Education Administration & Leadership, Teaching/Training & Facilitation, Learning Support & Community Engagement) — occupations include special education teacher, school administrator, school counselor, academic advisor, instructional coordinator, adult educator
- **Healthcare and Human Services** (sub-clusters: Behavioral & Mental Health, Biotechnology Research & Development, Community & Social Services, Health Data & Administration, Personal Care Services, Physical Health) — occupations include medical and health service managers, community health workers, registered nurses, nursing and physician assistants
- **Public Service and Safety** (sub-clusters: Emergency Response, Judicial Systems/Local/State/Federal Service, Military and National Security, Public Safety) — occupations include paralegal, firefighter, paramedic, police officer, crime scene analyst, transportation security officer, public health worker

## How AI is revolutionizing these occupations

**Education**: AI-powered learning platforms — Khan Academy's Khanmigo, Duolingo's adaptive algorithms, Quizlet — personalize instruction and provide real-time feedback. Superintendent Michael Matsuda (Southern California, grades 7–12) described using AI to synthesize student reflections structured around the "5 C's" (communication, collaboration, critical thinking, creativity, and compassion), creating feedback loops for both students and teachers that would not be feasible to generate manually given the volume of data involved. His district also uses eKanedce, a nonprofit digital platform supporting personalized learning paths and course management.

Dr. Edward Watson, Vice President for Digital Innovations at AAC&U and leader of the Institute on AI Pedagogy, described a fragmented campus policy landscape — some institutions deliberately avoid AI policy to preserve faculty innovation and instructional flexibility, delegating the decision down to individual syllabi or assignments, while others centralize it.

Khan Academy's Chief Learning Officer, Kristen DiCerbo, offered a skeptical counterpoint: comparing AI hype to prior predictions that MOOCs would eliminate higher education, she argued education is fundamentally human and requires interpersonal connection and mentorship that AI cannot substitute. Her framing: **"a human high five beats computer confetti."**

**Healthcare and Human Services**: AI systems improve diagnostic accuracy in imaging (radiologists must currently read images in just 3–4 seconds each given data volume; AI can flag concerns human review might miss). Yale New Haven Hospital's Rothman Index uses machine learning on aggregated patient documentation to assess risk and recommend care interventions. Duke University's School of Nursing and Engineering built the Tele-Robotic Intelligent Nursing Assistant (TRINA), which successfully completed 73% of assigned tasks in testing, though it remains significantly slower than human nurses and is an early-stage prototype (Li et al., 2017).

Nurses' documentation burden is substantial — Buchanan et al. (2020) found approximately one-third of nurses' workdays is consumed by paperwork — which AI-based note-taking is beginning to automate. Dr. Jose Bowen, Senior Scholar at AAC&U, proposed a pedagogical response to this shift: since AI will produce draft clinical notes, nursing education should shift from teaching students to *write* good notes toward teaching them to *recognize* good, bad, and "ugly" AI-generated notes — training the evaluative skill that remains necessary once AI produces the first draft.

Spartanburg Community College (South Carolina) is cited as one of the few postsecondary institutions proactively integrating AI-powered simulation software into nursing education, allowing students to practice on responsive virtual patients before working with live patients.

**Public Service and Safety**: ShotSpotter, an AI-predictive gunshot-detection system, is deployed in Chicago, Baltimore, and Miami-Dade, automatically triggering 911 dispatch and reducing response times. The Danish company Corti's AI assistant monitors emergency calls and detects cardiac arrests with 95% accuracy by analyzing speech patterns, background noise, and breathing irregularities. Machine learning can identify residential fire development stages with 85% accuracy (Fang et al., 2021). DARLENE, a European augmented-reality system, trains law enforcement officers in high-risk decision-making scenarios.

In legal education, Suffolk Law School has introduced three dedicated AI courses ("Generative AI and the Delivery of Legal Services," "Artificial Intelligence and the Law," "Emerging AI Regulatory Frameworks"). David Wilkins, Vice Dean at Harvard Law School's Center on the Legal Profession, observed that asking ChatGPT to draft a legal memo produces output "approximately as good as what a first-year law firm associate would produce" — and compared AI's effect on legal information access to how WebMD changed patients' relationship to medical information.

Commander Charlie Epperson of the U.S. Coast Guard's Office on Artificial Intelligence described AI's role in maritime search and rescue: automated sensors scanning large ocean areas for people in distress via computer vision, dramatically increasing coverage area beyond what human-operated sensors and observation could achieve, without replacing the judgment of the human operators directing the search.

## Will AI automate Caring for Communities occupations?

Applying the [[ai-occupational-exposure-index]] methodology, this report finds Caring for Communities occupations show **slightly higher than average AIOE exposure compared to the national landscape — with the exception of Healthcare and Human Services**, which tracks closer to the national average, especially at lower education levels. This makes Caring for Communities the highest-exposure grouping among the four studied in this report series.

Selected AIOE figures from the report's occupation tables: **Lawyers (1.33), Medical and health services managers (1.26), Middle school teachers (1.29), Secondary school teachers (1.19), Education administrators K–12 (1.05), Elementary school teachers (0.89)** all score well above the national mean. By contrast, **Postal service mail carriers (−1.06), Correctional officers and jailers (−0.89), Police and sheriff's patrol officers (−0.67), Childcare workers (−0.38), Nursing assistants (−0.27), Home health and personal care aides (−0.16)** score below it. Registered nurses (0.23) and preschool teachers (0.03) sit close to zero.

As in the other reports, higher AIOE is interpreted as likely AI *integration* rather than automatic displacement: "where there are higher AIOE scores, there is a higher likelihood that AI will be integrated into those fields of work." For the many bachelor's-degree-requiring Caring for Communities occupations with elevated scores, the report frames this as AI enhancing professional effectiveness (documentation support, diagnostic assistance, administrative efficiency) rather than replacing the underlying human judgment and interpersonal work.

## Recommendations

- **CTE providers — shift the narrative**: mandatory, not optional, AI exposure across every Caring for Communities program; take student data privacy seriously while addressing faculty concerns about educational quality; avoid the individuated, teacher-by-teacher policy model, which the report calls ineffective and inequitable. Cites the same Jensen Huang quote as the other reports in the series ("You are not going to lose your job to AI, you're going to lose your job to someone who uses AI") and the same finding that affluent-serving colleges are more likely to offer AI training than open-access institutions (Palmer, 2025).
- **Focus on skills, not only jobs**: prompt engineering and AI-output auditing should be added to the existing core of critical thinking, communication, and problem-solving skills already central to Caring for Communities occupations.
- **Industry partners**: bridge the AI knowledge gap — the report notes industry itself is often uncertain how to deploy AI, with one community college dean reporting he was regularly the most informed voice on AI in meetings with industry partners.
- **Policymakers — build on what works, invest in the future, and this is not a time for federal retreat**: leverage the Perkins V Comprehensive Local Needs Assessment; fund apprenticeships; continue investing in AI-enabled diagnostic technologies, AR/VR wearables, and robotics for classroom access. The report explicitly criticizes proposed Trump Administration/DOGE cuts to the Department of Labor (home to WIOA) and the stated goal of eliminating the Department of Education, arguing the evidence base for CTE's effectiveness (higher graduation rates, college enrollment, and wage premiums) makes this "not a time for federal retreat."

## Conclusion

The report situates its findings within a generational context: millennials and Gen Z learners are drawn to mission-oriented work, and the "college for all" mentality is waning as students grow skeptical of higher education's value proposition. Because Caring for Communities occupations are service- and mission-oriented, involve a balance of physical and cognitive tasks, and serve an aging population driving structural demand growth in healthcare and education, the report concludes these professions retain "tremendous appeal" and will remain largely relevant even as AI reshapes specific tasks within them. Workers combining technical skill, adaptability, and AI literacy are positioned to thrive.
