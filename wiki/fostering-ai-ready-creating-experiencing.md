# Fostering AI-Ready Creating & Experiencing CTE Pipelines

**Summary**: ERO/Advance CTE/ACTE report (August 2025) on AI's impact on the Creating & Experiencing Career Cluster Grouping — Hospitality/Events/Tourism and Arts/Entertainment/Design — notable for including a sharply dissenting interview voice (Jesse Anglen) whose ~100%-knowledge-worker-displacement prediction contradicts the report's own moderate conclusion and the wiki's dominant labor-market narrative.

## About this report

By Dani Rimbach-Jones, Cameron Sublett, Lauren Mason, Karina Beltran, & Matias Fresard. Education Research & Opportunity Center (ERO), University of Tennessee Knoxville, in partnership with Advance CTE and ACTE, funded by ECMC Foundation. August 2025. Final report in the four-part series covering four of the six modernized Career Cluster Groupings; see [[fostering-ai-ready-building-moving]], [[fostering-ai-ready-cultivating-resources]], and [[fostering-ai-ready-caring-communities]] for the other three, and [[ai-occupational-exposure-index]] for the shared methodology.

## What is the Creating & Experiencing cluster grouping?

The Creating & Experiencing grouping consists of two Career Clusters:

- **Hospitality, Events, & Tourism** (sub-clusters: Accommodations, Conferences & Events, Culinary & Food Service, Travel and Leisure) — occupations include hotel manager, food & beverage manager, event coordinator, concierge, travel guide, travel agent, chef
- **Arts, Entertainment, & Design** (sub-clusters: Design & Digital Arts, Fashion & Interiors, Fine Arts, Lighting & Sound Technology, Media Production & Broadcasting, Performing Arts) — occupations include graphic designer, talent agent, fashion & interior designer, museum curator, sound engineer, producer

## How AI is revolutionizing these occupations

**Hospitality, Events, & Tourism**: dynamic pricing algorithms (the same category as Uber's surge pricing) let hotels adjust room rates in real time based on demand, competitor pricing, and historical data. AI chatbots and virtual concierges (deployed by Hilton, The Cosmopolitan, and Marriott) provide 24/7 guest support. Duve, an AI hotel-operations platform, automatically triages guest communications by urgency and forecasts occupancy and maintenance needs. Automated cleaning systems (e.g., Placer Process Systems' Automated Clean In Place) handle food-and-beverage sanitation with reduced — not eliminated — human oversight. Event platforms like Motion optimize scheduling and registration workflows.

Daniel Doppler, President of QuickText, offered a framing that recurs throughout the report: "AI has to be connected to all the tools of the hotel... because an AI doesn't have any arms or any legs." He argued hospitality "is not fancy logic. Hospitality is hard logic," meaning AI must work *alongside* hospitality staff rather than independently, since it lacks the physical presence and situational judgment the work requires. He illustrated AI's personalization potential with an example: AI cross-referencing a traveler's flight booking against their Spotify playlist to autonomously surface a relevant concert (e.g., The Pixies) happening in their destination city during their stay.

The report characterizes the sector's current AI deployment as decision-support for guest-services staff (concierges, travel guides, travel agents) rather than replacement — but flags this as a *moderate current* exposure level with room to grow, not a durable ceiling.

**Arts, Entertainment, & Design**: generative platforms (DALL-E, RunwayML) accelerate concept art and illustration; CodeDesign.ai lets non-technical users build functional websites via AI-generated templates. In gaming, "Motion Learning" platforms let AI systems learn complex physical movements (from sports, martial arts, dance) and apply them to game character animation, replacing frame-by-frame manual animation with AI-assisted storyboarding. In film, AI tools now assist pre-production (storyboards, previsualization), production (visual effects, dialogue generation), and post-production (color grading, editing, AI-composed music scoring tailored to scene mood).

Robert Morton, Board Member of the Australian Cinematographers Society AI Committee, described the industry as still pre-mass-adoption: "We're not seeing it just yet. It's like that little thing just on the horizon... there's still a limited amount of AI artists." He named three commercial examples of AI-produced advertising he'd observed by mid-2025 (Toys 'R' Us, Coca-Cola's 2024 Christmas campaign, a KFC "spec ad" made without the brand's involvement), and predicted advertising would be "the first place to get tackled with AI" given its shorter format and faster production cycles.

A significant barrier to mass AI adoption in this cluster is energy cost: producing a single 5-second AI-generated video requires **3.7 million joules — equivalent to running a microwave for over an hour** (EVOTEK, 2025). Disney and other major studios use AI but largely behind closed doors, a caution stemming from contractual limits negotiated with the Writers Guild of America after the 2023 Writers Strike, in which AI use was a central bargaining issue.

## Will AI automate Creating & Experiencing occupations?

Applying the [[ai-occupational-exposure-index]] methodology, this grouping shows **average AI exposure relative to the national landscape, with an internal split**: Arts, Entertainment, & Design scores marginally *above* the national average (driven by cognitively-loaded, bachelor's-level creative and editorial roles), while Hospitality, Events, & Tourism scores slightly *below* average.

Selected AIOE figures: **Editors (1.13), Interior designers (1.12), Receptionists and information clerks (1.01), Producers and directors (0.94), Graphic designers (0.72)** score above the national mean. By contrast, **Landscaping and groundskeeping workers (−1.82), Food preparation workers and Maids/housekeeping cleaners (−1.23), Janitors and cleaners (−1.44), Waiters and waitresses (−0.95), Merchandise displayers/window trimmers (−1.00)** score well below it.

The report flags a notable paradox: occupations like graphic designers and interior designers show elevated AIOE scores despite requiring distinctly human creative judgment — high AI exposure at the task-composition level does not necessarily mean high risk of full automation when artistic sensibility remains a fundamental job requirement. It also raises a specific equity concern: because **women comprise 54% of the global tourism workforce** (World Tourism Organization, 2019) and the Hospitality, Events & Tourism cluster is flagged as "highly vulnerable to AI-driven automation" despite its below-average AIOE score, AI-driven displacement in this sector could disproportionately affect women workers — a gendered exposure dimension not raised in the other three reports in this series.

## A dissenting voice: Jesse Anglen

Unlike the other three reports in this series, this report includes one interview subject whose prediction directly contradicts the report's own concluding assessment. Jesse Anglen, CEO & Founder of Rapid Innovations, told the authors:

> "Globally, I believe that 52 trillion dollars is paid out in labor to people that are doing things... if AI can fully replace 60% of all knowledge workers, then that means that somewhere between 18 and 22 trillion dollars worth of what is currently being paid out to people in the labor market will disappear. So I believe AI will replace close to a hundred percent of knowledge workers in the next 5 years... I think that it is immoral to tell people 'Don't worry. There'll be new jobs created.' What they're taking that from is like the Industrial Revolution. [It] took a hundred years to go in and fundamentally turn the labor market upside down. This is not going to happen in a hundred years. We are not going to have time to create all of these new jobs. This is going to happen in 5 or 10 years."

The report's own Conclusion explicitly cites Anglen's framing — "we lack the century-long adaptation period that characterized previous technological shifts" — while still landing on a substantially more moderate final assessment: that Creating & Experiencing jobs "retain substantial relevance and human value," with AI serving primarily as a production tool rather than a wholesale replacement for human creative and hospitality judgment.

**This is a direct contradiction worth flagging against the wiki's dominant labor-market narrative.** Anglen's ~100%-of-knowledge-workers-in-5-years prediction stands in stark opposition to [[bcg-ai-reshapes-jobs]]'s 12%-Substituted figure, [[apocalypse-no-galloway]]'s "AI apocalypse is marketing" argument, [[ai-job-apocalypse-klein]]'s case for why mass AI unemployment probably won't happen, and the [[relational-economy]] thesis this wiki has built up across dozens of sources. Unlike those sources, Anglen's claim (a) is not tied to a named study or dataset, (b) comes from the CEO of an AI company with a direct commercial interest in the technology's perceived transformative power, and (c) is presented by the report's own authors as an outlier view rather than the report's conclusion. It is included here per the wiki's citation rule to note contradictions explicitly, not because the wiki's editors find it independently well-supported — no comparable empirical case for Anglen's 60%/100%/5-year figures is provided by the source document itself.

## Recommendations

The report follows the same four-audience structure as the other three: CTE providers should shift AI exposure from optional to mandatory across every Creating & Experiencing program while addressing student privacy and faculty concerns (citing the same Palmer 2025 finding on affluent-institution AI-training access advantage, and the same Jensen Huang quote as the other reports); focus on skills over job titles; industry partners should share concrete AI use cases; policymakers should leverage the Perkins V Comprehensive Local Needs Assessment, fund apprenticeships, and — as in [[fostering-ai-ready-caring-communities]] — the report explicitly warns against proposed federal cuts to CTE-supporting agencies (Department of Labor/WIOA, Department of Education) given the documented effectiveness of CTE outcomes.

## Conclusion

The report closes by arguing that the enduring human appeal of narrative and hospitality experiences — the reason people watch movies and travel — means AI will function as a production tool rather than a replacement for the underlying human element: "it is the spontaneous conversations with locals that create memorable experiences," and stories are brought to life "through the human artistry of screenwriters, actors, and filmmakers." This conclusion sits in tension with Anglen's framing quoted above, which the report acknowledges but does not resolve.
