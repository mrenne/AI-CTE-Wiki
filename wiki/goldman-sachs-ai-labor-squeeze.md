# Goldman Sachs: Where AI Is Squeezing Labor Markets

**Summary**: A CNBC report (August 2026) on a Goldman Sachs research note finding AI-related hiring headwinds across developed economies — call-center employment down 27-39% below trend across the US, Canada, and Germany, entry-level workers bearing 2-6x the drag on hiring growth that the broader workforce experiences — the wiki's first cross-country, investment-bank-sourced corroboration of its US-only ADP/Anthropic labor-market findings.

**Sources**: `raw/misc/Goldman studied where AI is squeezing labor markets. Here's what it found.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-08-23

---

## About this source

CNBC report (Jenny Lee, August 19, 2026) summarizing a Goldman Sachs research note published the same week. Goldman analyzed employment growth across more than 800 occupations and combined 11 surveys of AI adoption to produce a multi-country view of where AI-related labor-market pressure is showing up — the wiki's first non-US-only occupational exposure evidence, and the first sourced from a sell-side investment bank rather than an academic or think-tank research program.

## Key findings

**Industry-level employment has fallen sharply below trend in the most AI-exposed sectors, especially call centers.** Goldman's four flagged industries — call centers, software publishing, management consulting, and advertising services — all show employment below their historical trend across developed markets. Call centers are the most extreme case: employment is **39% below trend in the US, 33% below trend in Canada, and 27% below trend in Germany**. Goldman explicitly frames this as evidence that "AI-related employment pressures are already visible in industries where tools capable of automating work are available" — a direct, occupation-level confirmation of the automation-mechanism thesis already documented in [[canaries-coal-mine-brynjolfsson]] and [[stanford-del-ai-economic-indicators]], now replicated outside the US.

**Entry-level workers bear a disproportionate share of the drag — a specific multiplier, not just a qualitative pattern.** Across the broader labor market, a 10% increase in occupational AI exposure is associated with only a 0.1 percentage-point drag on annual headcount growth in France, Canada, and the US. For entry-level workers specifically, the same 10% exposure increase drags headcount growth by **0.2 to 0.6+ percentage points** (lowest in the US, highest in Australia) — a **2x to 6x multiplier** concentrated at the entry-level tier. This is a more precise, cross-country-comparable version of the entry-level concentration finding the wiki has documented domestically via [[canaries-coal-mine-brynjolfsson]] (19% kept-pace shortfall) and [[anthropic-labor-market-index]] (14% hiring-rate decline).

**A separate, smaller displacement-risk effect layers on top of the exposure effect.** Goldman finds an additional negative effect specifically among occupations rated high-risk for AI displacement, distinct from and smaller than the general exposure effect — suggesting occupational exposure and displacement risk are related but not identical constructs, each contributing independently to hiring headwinds.

**AI adoption is already broad, not niche, across developed economies.** Combining 11 surveys, Goldman estimates developed-market AI adoption at roughly **15-20%**, with France, the US, the Netherlands, and the UK leading and Italy, Japan, and New Zealand lagging. Major emerging markets trail at 10-15%. This adoption-rate context matters for reading the employment findings: the labor-market effects being documented are occurring at adoption rates still well under a majority of the workforce, implying further headwinds are plausible as adoption continues to rise.

## Why this matters for the wiki

This is the wiki's **first cross-country corroboration of its (previously US-only) labor-market evidence base**, from a source with no academic or advocacy stake in the outcome. It closes part of the external-validity gap the wiki has flagged repeatedly — [[ai-economy-measurement-2026]] notes that US private data sources (ADP, Ramp, Revelio) conflict with each other and with national survey data; [[canaries-coal-mine-brynjolfsson]]'s own August 2026 update found its ADP-observed magnitude was larger than the comparable ACS figure. Goldman's finding — directionally consistent with the ADP/Anthropic pattern, replicated across Germany, Australia, Canada, France, and the UK using a different data source and methodology entirely — is independent evidence for the *direction* of the effect (automation-heavy occupations show entry-level hiring headwinds) even though it says nothing about resolving the *magnitude* debate.

## Implications for CTE

1. **The entry-level concentration finding is now internationally replicated, not a US-data artifact.** CTE programs preparing students for AI-exposed white-collar entry points (customer service, software-adjacent roles) should treat the hiring headwind as a structural, cross-country pattern rather than an ADP-sample-specific finding.
2. **Call centers are the most extreme case documented anywhere in the wiki** — a 27-39% below-trend decline is a larger effect than any single-occupation figure previously in the evidence base, and gives CTE programs a concrete example of what "automation-type AI usage" (the [[canaries-coal-mine-brynjolfsson]] mechanism) looks like in realized employment data.
3. **15-20% AI adoption in developed economies means the labor-market effects documented so far are early-stage, not mature.** This reinforces the wiki's recurring caution (see [[ai-economy-measurement-2026]]) against reading current employment data as a ceiling on AI's eventual labor-market impact.
