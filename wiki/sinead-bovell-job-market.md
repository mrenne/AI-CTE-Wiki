## The Core Claim: Task Unbundling

Bovell's central analytical contribution is the concept of **task unbundling**: AI does not automate jobs — it automates tasks within jobs. The distinction matters because it determines what happens to existing roles.

> "AI isn't capable of automating an entire workflow. At least not yet... But what it can do is automate tasks within a job."

Her worked example: a financial analyst whose ten daily tasks include weekly financial reports, cash flow flagging, variance analysis, and client-facing strategy presentations. If AI handles six of those ten tasks effectively, what happens to the role?

The answer is not elimination — it's evolution. The remaining four tasks (which AI cannot yet handle well) become the core of a new, higher-level role: **financial strategist**. The person still works; the work looks different. Job titles stop tracking what people actually do. (source: The Job Market Is Going Away.md)

This is a more granular and narrative articulation of what the [[bcg-ai-reshapes-jobs]] taxonomy formalizes as the "Divergent" segment — roles where AI automates junior/routine tasks while senior judgment-and-strategy work persists and becomes the core of the redefined role.

## Skills > Job Titles

As task unbundling accelerates, fixed job descriptions become unreliable signals. The workforce reorganizes around skills rather than titles:

> "The future becomes much more about skills than about jobs."

The key insight is that the dominant skill set within any role shifts as AI takes over certain tasks:

**Before AI**: financial analyst = best number-cruncher wins  
**After AI**: financial analyst = best problem-framer, trade-off evaluator, and communicator wins

> "The skills that made you dominant before AI may not be the same skills that make you dominant after AI."

This is not a claim that technical skills become worthless — it's a claim that the configuration of skills required for competitive differentiation changes as AI automates the most procedural layer. The skills left are precisely [[transferable-skills]]: communication, critical thinking, judgment, ethical reasoning, and problem-framing. (source: The Job Market Is Going Away.md)

## The Independence Era: Rise of Entrepreneurial Work

As roles grow more fluid and skills more project-specific, Bovell predicts a structural shift toward **independent contracts** over full-time employment:

> "Is a company going to hire for a full-time role or much more likely to hire an independent contract because they know that the person who's best for the tasks that need to be completed today may not have the skills for the task that need to be completed in a couple years."

The result is what Bovell calls the **independence era**:

- Workers offer their skills to multiple clients simultaneously (e.g., 40% FTE with one company, 40% with another, with independent contracts filling the gap)
- Work becomes **project-based and entrepreneurial** rather than role-based and institutional
- Workers must continually update their skills to remain competitive across shifting client needs
- Lifelong learning becomes a structural economic requirement, not a career preference

This is a specific structural prediction that complements the [[relational-economy]] thesis: not only do relational sectors grow, but the *form* of work across the economy shifts from employment to independent contracting — making the skills that allow you to market, demonstrate, and continuously develop your capabilities more important than any single technical credential. (source: The Job Market Is Going Away.md)

## The Transition Risk: Jobs Disappearing Faster Than Work Emerges

Bovell separates long-run equilibrium from medium-term transition risk — a distinction that often gets collapsed in AI-labor debates.

**Long run**: New work will emerge. 60% of current workers hold occupations that didn't exist 80 years ago (software, podcasting, social media management, most of modern finance). The internet made distribution and communication cheap; the economy reorganized around that. AI will make cognitive production cheap; the economy will reorganize again.

**Medium run**: The transition may not be smooth.

> "Is it possible that jobs disappear faster than new types of work emerge? Yes, that is entirely possible. And we've seen this with past technological transformations."

This is the honest acknowledgment missing from optimistic takes: historical structural transitions have sometimes produced extended unemployment between the destruction of old work and the creation of new work. If the AI transition compresses that timeline, or if the new work requires skill profiles that displaced workers can't acquire rapidly, the social cost could be severe.

Her policy implication: the answer must be developed **now** — before the transition peaks. Health insurance decoupled from employment, portable benefits, and active skill-development infrastructure are prerequisites for a participatory (rather than exclusionary) transition. (source: The Job Market Is Going Away.md)

This aligns with the [[anthropic-labor-market-index]] finding of a 14% hiring slowdown for workers aged 22–25 entering AI-exposed occupations — early evidence of the transition risk manifesting at the entry point of the labor market.

## Power Asymmetry as Structural Threat

Bovell frames private-sector optimization as a structural threat that requires government counterweight:

> "The private sector is optimized for profit and shareholder value — not necessarily the humans within the labor market... If we don't see that type of restructuring, that type of support from government, that type of regulation that actually curbs extreme power asymmetry, we might have a future where companies really just kind of see us as just dispensable short-term pieces of labor."

This is a stronger structural critique than most labor-market sources in the wiki. It doesn't accept that market forces will automatically produce a good outcome for workers — it identifies the absence of worker bargaining power as a structural problem that policy must address. Without deliberate design:

- Workers have no leverage in an independent-contract economy
- Benefits and protections tied to full-time employment erode
- The prosperity generated by AI accrues to capital, not labor

> "Everybody should be skilled and empowered to participate in how work is going to evolve and have a say in how it gets shaped. Not just some far out UBI type of strategy today."

The critique extends to cognitive labor: "It's our cognitive data and our cognitive labor that AI is being trained on. So everybody should be able to take part in the prosperity that AI is generating today." (source: The Job Market Is Going Away.md)

## AI Literacy as the New Baseline

Bovell converges with [[stanford-ai-index-education-2026]] and [[applied-co-intelligence-report]] on the direction of AI literacy, adding a memorable frame:

> "AI is not going to get the right answer the first time... You can think of AI in your role like an intern."

The intern analogy unpacks what AI literacy actually requires:
- **Frame the problem properly** — don't just say "do this thing" and expect results
- **Evaluate its work** — an intern's output needs checking
- **Give feedback** — the first attempt is not the final product
- **Understand the boundaries** — know what would and wouldn't be in its knowledge/data

This is pedagogically useful because it grounds abstract AI literacy advice in a social relationship most workers understand. The analogy implicitly invokes [[transferable-skills]]: coaching, communication, critical evaluation, and judgment are what the "manager of an AI intern" needs — not just technical prompting knowledge.

## Emerging Roles: AI Agent Architects

Bovell documents an early example of genuinely new work emerging from AI:

> "Being able to build AI agents, and these are systems that can take action on their own... AI agent architect, whatever you may call it — so already new types of jobs are starting to appear."

Her example: a person who builds automated workflows for a venture capital firm — pulling financial spreadsheets, building decks, routing to the right VP, triggering invoices from accounting when thresholds are hit. This is a role that requires understanding both the business logic and the AI tooling, without being either a traditional software engineer or a traditional analyst. New work for new tasks.

This supports the long-run argument against mass unemployment: the AI era is already generating occupational categories that didn't exist, at the very moment it's reshaping existing ones. (source: The Job Market Is Going Away.md)
