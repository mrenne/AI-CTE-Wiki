# AI, Jobs, and the Next Generation

**Summary**: A June 2026 Microsoft blog post by Brad Smith (President) responding to 2026 college graduates booing AI at commencement speeches — the first tech-industry-insider voice in the wiki. Provides Microsoft's own AI diffusion data, argues the pace of disruption is constrained by institutional change not technology, and names the dignity of work as a first-order value the tech sector must protect.

**Sources**: `raw/blog/AI, jobs, and the next generation.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-06-10

## Source Context

Brad Smith is President and Vice Chairman of Microsoft. This post appeared on the official Microsoft corporate blog on June 10, 2026, written in direct response to the wave of anti-AI sentiment at 2026 college commencement ceremonies across the United States.

**Source type caveat**: This is a corporate blog post by a tech industry executive with a stake in the AI narrative. The Microsoft AI Diffusion Report figures come from Microsoft's own research. Smith's arguments are simultaneously those of a longtime industry observer with real institutional knowledge and an interested party. Both dimensions matter when reading this source. (source: AI, jobs, and the next generation.md)

## The Student Backlash as a 2026 Signal

Smith opens with what he calls a "powerful wake-up call for the tech sector": 2026 university graduates booing AI at commencement addresses and rejecting AI-generated work in favor of explicitly human creation.

**The Princeton beer jacket story**: Princeton seniors traditionally design a "beer jacket" for graduation. This year, a student petition successfully rejected a popular design because it had been created with AI assistance. The replacement read: "100% cotton — 100% human."

Smith's interpretation:
> "The rejection of artificial fibers and artificial intelligence illustrates how human tastes shape market economics even as efficiency and productivity advance. Machines don't buy products. People do."

The backlash is structurally significant because of who is doing it. A new Microsoft study shows that counties with large college towns and populations aged 18–24 have the *highest* AI adoption rates in the United States — young people are the most enthusiastic AI users and the most vocally concerned about its impact. When a generation's leading adopters are also its most vocal critics, the signal is harder to dismiss as technophobia. (source: AI, jobs, and the next generation.md)

This complements the [[nyt-teens-trade-classes]] behavioral evidence: both document the class of 2026 processing AI's impact in real time — the NYT piece through trade enrollment decisions, this piece through commencement sentiment and clothing symbolism.

## Microsoft AI Diffusion Report: Current Adoption Baseline

Smith cites Microsoft's own AI Diffusion Report (US, Q1 2026) for the current state of AI adoption:

- **17.8%** of the world's working-age population currently uses generative AI
- **31.3%** in the United States — higher than the global average but still less than one in three workers
- Counties with large college towns and ages 18–24 have the highest US adoption rates

These are the most current large-scale adoption estimates available in the wiki. The numbers contextualize the "AI is already everywhere" claim: at 31.3%, the US is well short of majority adoption even among the most technology-saturated workforce in the world.

**The diffusion pace argument** (Narayanan & Kapoor, Knight Columbia, cited by Smith):
> "Diffusion is limited by the speed of human, organizational, and institutional change."

This directly challenges rapid-disruption timelines. AI's capacity to automate tasks is not the binding constraint on economic transformation — the binding constraint is how quickly humans, organizations, and institutions can absorb and reorganize around those capabilities. Smith applies this to his own organization: "broad AI transformation over the next quarter century would itself be remarkable." That is a deliberately measured claim from the president of a company with enormous financial stakes in accelerating AI adoption. (source: AI, jobs, and the next generation.md)

## "Perfect Storm" Headwinds for 2026 Graduates

Smith is unusually candid about the multiple simultaneous pressures facing 2026 graduates:

> "They face multiple headwinds as they enter the job market. This includes AI automation of tasks in current entry-level positions and, especially in the tech sector, corporate pressure to reduce headcount to help pay for AI's enormous capital expenditures. It also involves other factors, including geopolitical uncertainty, trade tensions, and correction from over-hiring in the early years of the decade. Like a perfect storm, the wind is blowing from multiple directions."

This is the most honest "it's bad right now for new graduates" statement from a tech industry executive in the wiki. It explicitly names the mechanism that other sources only imply: companies are cutting headcount partly to fund AI capex — not primarily because AI has made those workers unnecessary, but because the AI buildout requires massive capital reallocation. The [[broken-ladder]] WFH-confound analysis and [[anthropic-labor-market-index]] hiring slowdown data are corroborated here from an industry-insider perspective.

Smith also names the COVID-era over-hiring correction as a compounding factor — consistent with the Galloway ([[apocalypse-no-galloway]]) layoff anatomy (Meta returning to 2021 headcount, Microsoft 47% larger than pre-pandemic). The "perfect storm" framing captures the multi-causal reality better than any single-variable analysis. (source: AI, jobs, and the next generation.md)

## The Task-Bucket Framework: Individual Guidance

Drawing from Ryan Roslansky (LinkedIn CEO) and Aneesh Ramen's book *Open to Work: How to Get Ahead in the Age of AI* (2026), Smith offers the most practically actionable version of task unbundling in the wiki:

> "Think about your job not as a title but a bundle of tasks. Write down a list of your tasks and put them into three buckets: the bucket of tasks that AI can do; the bucket of tasks that you can do with AI; and the bucket of tasks that humans must do by themselves."

The three buckets:
1. **AI can do it alone** — if almost everything is here, consider a different type of job
2. **You + AI** — where most tasks land for most workers; focus attention and productivity here
3. **Humans only** — irreplaceable; protect and develop this capability

**The strategic implication**: If bucket 1 tasks are handled by AI, the worker can direct more attention to buckets 2 and 3 — becoming more productive and more distinctively valuable simultaneously. The framing is explicitly not about replacement but about reallocation of human attention to higher-value work.

This is the [[sinead-bovell-job-market]] task unbundling concept made explicit and actionable as a personal career-planning exercise. Bovell's financial analyst → financial strategist narrative is the same mechanism; Roslansky/Ramen give it a concrete diagnostic structure. (source: AI, jobs, and the next generation.md)

## The Five Cs: A New Transferable Skills Taxonomy

Roslansky and Ramen name five "soft skills that are uniquely human" in the AI era — all beginning with C:

1. **Curiosity** — the drive to ask deeper questions and pursue understanding beyond the AI's output
2. **Creativity** — synthesis, originality, and vision that generic AI cannot replicate
3. **Compassion** — empathic engagement with other humans, inseparable from care, education, and service contexts
4. **Communications** — translating AI-generated insight into human action and persuasion
5. **Courage** — making difficult calls, advocating for positions, taking accountable ownership of outcomes

Smith's addition: "Even when AI automates multiple tasks, people must continue to oversee its work. This creates the need for additional human observation and insight. In short, human judgment remains essential."

The Five Cs are convergent with but distinct from the [[applied-co-intelligence-model]]'s transferable skills framework (which emphasizes critical thinking, ethical reasoning, problem-solving, and collaboration) and the BCG framework (human interaction intensity and low process structure). All three frameworks identify a similar cluster of distinctively human capacities from different entry points — labor economics, learning science, and management consulting.

**Note on source**: The Five Cs are attributed to Roslansky and Ramen at LinkedIn, not to Microsoft's analysis. Smith is relaying their framework with endorsement. (source: AI, jobs, and the next generation.md)

## The Dignity of Work: A Normative Argument

The most distinctive contribution of this source to the wiki — a normative argument about *why* the stakes are high, not just an empirical claim about what will happen:

> "The American Dream has been founded on the dignity of work and the critical role it plays in giving life purpose. Great countries are built on great economies and great jobs. To those in the tech sector who seemingly want to pursue a future where computers replace jobs and AI becomes more capable than people, the next generation of people has offered a compelling response: 'not so fast.'"

And:

> "The technological, economic, and societal transformations of the past three decades have left too many people behind. We'll need to try different approaches, built on more shared responsibilities, if we're going to do better as we move forward."

This is the only source in the wiki where a tech industry executive explicitly acknowledges that the previous wave of technological transformation failed large portions of the population — and argues from within the tech sector that this outcome must not repeat. The dignity-of-work framing moves beyond economic productivity (AI should generate growth) and equity (benefits should be shared) to something more fundamental: work as a source of purpose that AI must not undermine even as it transforms.

Smith's normative position explicitly endorses the students' backlash: the next generation is right to insist that the future be decided by humans, with broad community input rather than a "narrow group of elites." This is an unusual statement from the president of one of the companies making those decisions. (source: AI, jobs, and the next generation.md)

## Corporate Data Sovereignty

Smith extends the data sovereignty argument — which in [[ai-unplugged-murgatroyd]] focuses on indigenous and national sovereignty — to a competitive imperative for every organization:

> "The benefits of AI for a business will be short-lived if it transfers and trains someone else's AI model using a firm's unique knowledge and expertise. This helps explain why each company needs to develop its own internal AI capabilities and control its own data."

> "Sovereignty must be preserved not only for countries but for companies. And privacy must be protected not only for individuals but for organizations."

The mechanism: an organization whose proprietary knowledge is used to train a third-party AI model loses the competitive advantage that knowledge represents. As AI diffuses, the organizations that retain control over their unique data and expertise retain their differentiator; those that cede data control cede their edge.

This extends [[ai-privacy-and-institutional-adoption]]'s privacy and sovereignty framing from schools/communities to the entire commercial economy — and from a regulatory compliance concern to a strategic competitive concern. (source: AI, jobs, and the next generation.md)

## Historical Parallels

Smith deploys the camera/painting analogy at length (photography was predicted to kill painting; instead it freed painting to become expressionist, impressionist, cubist) and the Jevons Paradox variants: word processing didn't eliminate typing jobs, spreadsheets didn't reduce financial work, email increased communication frequency. These are convergent with [[ai-job-apocalypse-klein]]'s accountants-after-VisiCalc argument but stated as direct Microsoft corporate experience over 50 years.

His principle: "When technology increases supply, human ambition often generates more demand." This is the Jevons Paradox as organizational experience, not economic theory. (source: AI, jobs, and the next generation.md)
