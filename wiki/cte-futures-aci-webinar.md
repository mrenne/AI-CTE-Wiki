## Overview

*Preparing CTE Learners for an AI-Driven Workforce: Introducing an Applied Co-Intelligence Model* is a February 23, 2026 webinar hosted by CTE Futures, ACTE, and Advance CTE. Dr. Cameron Sublett (Foundation for California Community Colleges / CTE Futures) presents the [[applied-co-intelligence-report]] and [[applied-co-intelligence-model]], joined by:

- **Lauren Mason** — CTE Futures
- **Alisha Hyslop** — ACTE chief policy officer
- **Kate Kreamer** — Advance CTE executive director
- **Michael Tinsley** — Tennessee Board of Regents
- **Dr. Tom Pigg** — Jackson State Community College

This webinar functions as a video companion to the written report, with the added value of live practitioner perspectives on barriers and real-world conditions.

---

## Core Framing: Skill Economy, Not Job Economy

Sublett opens with a precise framing of where AI disruption stands as of early 2026:

> "AI is reshaping the skill economy, not necessarily the job economy yet."

The word "yet" is deliberate. AI is currently reorganizing *which skills matter within jobs* rather than eliminating jobs outright — but the trajectory continues. This framing positions CTE's challenge as a curriculum and credential design problem in the near term, not merely a labor market survival problem.

---

## Technology Is Not Destiny: ACI as Countervailing Force

The webinar explicitly grounds the ACI model in Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson's *Power and Progress* (2023):

> "Technology is not destiny. The path that technology takes is a result of human choices and countervailing forces. The ACI model is our countervailing force."

This moves the framework from descriptive (here is how AI affects CTE) to political-economic (here is how CTE can actively shape AI's trajectory in the workforce). Acemoglu and Johnson's core argument is that technology's effects on workers depend on which institutions and stakeholders make choices about how it is deployed — and that labor-market outcomes historically favorable to workers have required deliberate countervailing action. The ACI model positions CTE institutions as that countervailing actor in the current transition.

See [[applied-co-intelligence-model]] for the framework itself.

## National Career Clusters Framework Update

Kate Kreamer (Advance CTE executive director) reports a significant structural update to the National Career Clusters Framework:

**Digital Technology has been added as a crosscutting cluster**, alongside Health Science and Education. Critically, its design intent is different from a standard cluster: it is meant to be **integrated across every other pathway sector**, not taught as a standalone technical track. A data science and AI subcluster sits within it.

This is a structural signal from the national CTE policy apparatus: AI and digital technology are not a separate CTE domain but a capability layer that cuts through every occupational field — healthcare AI tools, AI-assisted construction planning, AI-enabled hospitality personalization. The cluster architecture embeds the ACI model's integration imperative into the organizing framework for CTE nationally.

## CLNA as Implementation Vehicle

A concrete policy mechanism surfaces in the webinar: the **Comprehensive Local Needs Assessment (CLNA)** — required under the Perkins V federal funding framework — is identified as the existing vehicle for bridging the provider/employer alignment gap.

The CLNA requires CTE programs to assess workforce needs, engage employers, and document gaps. Rather than building new structures to accomplish the continuous employer-partnership work the ACI model requires, programs can repurpose this existing mandate. Practitioners note that most programs currently treat the CLNA as a compliance exercise rather than a strategic tool; reframing it as the vehicle for ongoing AI curriculum alignment would require no new authority — only changed practice.

## Traditional Partnership Models Are Broken

A panel consensus finding: annual advisory board meetings and typical curriculum review cycles cannot keep up with AI's rate of change. As Tinsley and Pigg both note, by the time a program completes a standard curriculum revision cycle (often 18–24 months), the AI landscape it was responding to has changed.

The structural implication is that CTE's historic institutional agility — fast advisory boards, employer-aligned pathways — only delivers its advantage if partnerships operate on *continuous* feedback loops rather than annual review cycles. Employers are not passive guides waiting for annual meetings; they are also actively confused about where AI is headed and need CTE as a genuine partner in working it out, not a recipient of occasional employer input.

## Practitioner Voices on the Four Obstacles

The panel confirms all four obstacles from the [[applied-co-intelligence-report]] with live institutional detail:

### 1. Alignment Crisis
Both educators and employers are waiting to understand where AI is going before committing. Tinsley notes that employers "are confused too" about AI direction — the alignment failure is symmetric. Neither side has enough certainty to drive clear curriculum mandates.

### 2. Human Capital Gap
Faculty need time, tools, and institutional support to develop AI integration competence. Over 90% of CTE coordinators in Tennessee reported needing support — but the gap is not motivation, it is institutional provision. Pigg notes that incumbent workers are *actively requesting* AI education, which creates an urgent demand signal that most programs are not yet equipped to meet.

### 3. Structural Barriers
Fragmented policies across districts and systems; some programs banning AI while others have no guidance; digital equity gaps in rural and low-income schools. The lack of binding state standards (documented in [[stanford-ai-index-education-2026]]) means programs operate without a policy floor.

### 4. Evidence Gap
No longitudinal studies on how AI integration affects CTE retention, skill transfer, or wage outcomes. Without evidence, both educators and policymakers are making consequential decisions in the dark.

## "AI Is Not Going to Fix It for You"

A recurring practitioner theme: students must understand where AI breaks down, not just where it helps. As Pigg puts it, AI struggles in context-rich environments that require physical presence, sustained judgment, and rapid adaptation to variability — exactly the environments CTE students will work in.

AI literacy that only teaches students to use AI effectively misses a necessary second component: understanding AI's failure modes in high-stakes occupational contexts. A nurse whose documentation AI misfires, an electrician whose diagnostic AI misreads a non-standard installation, a teacher whose AI coaching tool gives advice inappropriate for a specific student — these are not edge cases. They are the normal conditions of relational, physical, variable work.

This reinforces the [[agentivism]] framework's emphasis on epistemic monitoring: practitioners cannot benefit from AI collaboration unless they can reliably detect when the AI is wrong.

---

## Stand-Alone AI Courses as Insufficient

Tom Pigg is direct: stand-alone AI courses are not enough. Students (and incumbent workers) must encounter AI *within their occupational context* to understand what it can and cannot do in their specific field. A generic "AI literacy" course teaches prompting; it cannot develop the contextual judgment required to audit AI output in a healthcare setting or a skilled trades environment.

This reaffirms the [[applied-co-intelligence-model]]'s core design principle: AI Mastery must develop through Technical Skills and Transferable Skills simultaneously, in occupational context — not as a prior or parallel track.
